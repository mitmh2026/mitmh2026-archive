    Solution to PixelPinpointr

    Answer: FLATWISE
    Written by: Kevin Irish and Yujie Wan
    Edited by: Dan Simon

    This is a “GeoGuessr in video games” puzzle. The high-level solve path involves:

    1. Initial breakthrough (identifying the GeoGuessr and video game elements);
    2. Identify the game and the in-game viewpoint location for each panorama entry;
    3. Identify each game’s region on the combined map;
    4. Draw the path connecting all viewpoint locations (in the correct order, with straight lines between locations) on the combined map;
    5. Extract the initials from the location names at path intersections (in the correct order), and get the puzzle solution.

    Initial breakthrough

    The title “PixelPinpointr” hints at the GeoGuessr theme. It should be obvious that the panoramas are from video game worlds, and “pixel” is related to video games. “Pinpointr” also hints that the puzzle requires relatively precise identification of the locations.

    The “5,000 units” in the final description is another gentle confirmation that GeoGuessr mechanics are in-play, as a perfect (accurate) score in GeoGuessr nets 5,000 points.

    Identify viewpoint locations

    Entry 1

    Entry 1 is from Fallout 4. “Commonwealth” in the text points to Fallout 4 (Fallout 4 is set in a post-apocalyptic Boston and the surrounding Massachusetts region, known in-game as "the Commonwealth").

    The MIT Dome (known as “CIT Rotunda” in-game) in the panorama helps to locate the viewpoint’s general area (Cambridge, near the Institute). Further researching notable locations near CIT Rotunda on a Fallout 4 map will lead to Kendall hospital, the architectural details of which match the building shown in the panorama right next to the viewpoint.



    Entry 2

    Entry 2 is from Grand Theft Auto V. “Should have taken a car” in the text points to the Grand Theft Auto franchise, and Vinewood (the “Vinewood” sign is visible in the panorama) is a prominent GTA V location.

    Using the “Hearty Taco” and “Radio Mirror Park” signs in the panorama, we can find the address of Hearty Taco restaurant’s Mirror Park location (Bridge Street and West Mirror Drive, Mirror Park) and locate it on a GTA V map.

    Using additional clues in the panorama (e.g. the “Vinewood” sign and mountain, IAA tower, etc.), we could further determine the relative direction between Hearty Taco and the viewpoint, and thus find out the location of the viewpoint.



    Entry 3

    Entry 3 is from The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild. “Link” in the text refers to Link, the main character of the Zelda series; and “wilderness” further hints at the Breath of the Wild game in the series.

    Image searching of the scenery inside the wall should lead to the Great Plateau and Temple of Time, which helps to locate the viewpoint’s general area (Great Plateau). Using additional clues in the panorama like the shrine (Oman Au Shrine) and the tower (Great Plateau Tower) as well as the Hyrule Castle and the Death Mountain in the distance, we should be able to find the exact location of the viewpoint on a BotW map.



    Entry 4

    Entry 4 is from The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim. “Made like an arrow” and “knee-deep” in the text allude to the “arrow in the knee” Skyrim meme.

    Image searching the stone structure on the snow mountain should lead to Bleak Falls Barrow; Using Bleak Falls Barrow and the other snow peak in the panorama, we could locate the viewpoint’s general area on a Skyrim map. The island in the lake (with a boat on the island) should help pinpoint the exact location of the viewpoint.



    Entry 5

    Entry 5 is from World of Warcraft, hinted by “Wow!” in the text.

    Image searching the panorama should lead to Westfall. Looking at Westfall on a WoW map, the bridge shown in the panorama could only either be the bridge between Westfall and Elwynn Forest, or the bridge between Westfall and Duskwood. Further research could confirm it is the bridge to Duskwood (the Elwynn Forest bridge is a stone structure).

    Note locating the viewpoint should be feasible whether we are looking at a classic or retail version of the WoW map (the panorama was captured on classic).



    Entry 6

    Entry 6 is from Red Dead Redemption 2. “Horse around” in the text points to the RDR franchise.

    Image searching the bridge in the panorama (or just searching for bridges in RDR) should lead to Bard’s Crossing. Using other clues from the panorama (the river, the island, the nearby road triangle, etc.), we could pinpoint the viewpoint location near Bard’s Crossing on a RDR2 map.



    Entry 7

    Entry 7 is from The Witcher 3. “A few rounds of cards” (which refers to Gwent) in the text hints at Witcher. Additionally, “heart of stone” was added to the text because the viewpoint location is at Miller’s Lake, which is released in the Heart of Stone expansion.

    Searching for windmills in Witcher 3 should lead to Upper Mill. Combining its location on a Witcher 3 map with the lake and the two islands in the panorama, we will be able to locate the viewpoint.



    Identify game regions on combined map

    The map image in the puzzle combines road networks from relevant regions in all 7 game maps. Water and snow mountain features were added to help identify the game regions.



    Draw path connecting viewpoint locations

    The texts shown with the panorama entries indicate that this is a sequential journey between these locations, and emphasize that straight lines are being drawn/traversed between them. The map interaction tool only allows drawing straight lines between the pin marks, which is another nudge towards straight paths.

    The measure distance tool and the total distance traveled mentioned in the text provide a checksum for the viewpoint identifications.



    Extraction

    The final text suggests that something needs to be extracted from each intersection point. “Every time I crossed my own path” indicates that the intersections are extracted in the order when intersections occur as the path is being drawn.



    Overlaying the intersection points on the corresponding game maps, we will find a named point of interest at each intersection. The emojis provide another confirmation for the intersection POIs.

    Author’s Notes

    We’ve had the idea for “GeoGuessr in video games” kicking around for some time; there always seemed to be natural synergy there. Then, on the plane flight back from MITMH 2025, we both had decided our most enjoyable puzzle experience of the weekend had been the lovely “Where Am I?”, and we immediately started talking about the feasibility of a video game-themed follow-up. Only after we started working on a construction did we discover that this was an actual thing: lostgamer and Game Guessr being some prominent implementations. Oh well, we decided to press on ahead anyway!

    Formulation

    The choice of the games themselves was fairly arbitrary, but we knew we wanted games that were decently popular and had enough resources online that researching locations without prior knowledge was plausible. Additionally, the games needed a sufficiently large main map in order to be interesting, as well as a canonical map representation (with canonically-named locations on the map) to make for a good extraction. For many of the games we considered, the unique + canonical map locations ended up being the weakest link (no Subnautica or Assassin’s Creed, sorry.). Of the seven we ended up with, one of us had played World of Warcraft for several hundred hours, the other had played Skyrim for a couple hundred hours, and we had virtually no time in any of the others. Many Steam purchases were made and tutorials were speed-run in order to get to the final form.

    Deciding on how to turn a bunch of location guesses into an integrated puzzle with a satisfying extraction was one of the more difficult parts of the process. Many of the earlier drafts involved much lengthier (and perhaps less inspired) extractions: some involved semaphores based on the compass direction of the initial view, and some involved just identifying the closest named location. Some strategies imposed a sorting step: sometimes it was the release year of the game - one time it was the time-of-in-game-day as derived based on the sun’s location in the panorama. Phew, glad we abandoned that! Only once the idea of stitching multiple maps together (to act as a sort of cartographic "intersection letter soup") bubbled up did things begin to crystallize.

    The alignment of the individual maps on the master map took a lot of iterations to to get something that felt good solving. Some earlier versions required too much precision, lest the intersection points became too ambiguous in POI-dense regions of their respective maps (or straddling map boundaries). The relative scale of each map also fluctuated significantly as we tried to make certain angles work (we did manage to keep ourselves to the constraint of not changing the north orientation for any of the maps).     Technical Development

    The map took a lot (a lot) of time to draw, as every feature (street / highway / water edge / mountain contour) was composed, by hand, by tracing paths in Inkscape on top of the reference maps, and then manually munging the SVG output to optimize for the site implementation. The panoramas also ended up being unexpectedly time-consuming, as the initial plan (using Nvidia’s Ansel to take 360° snapshots) fell apart when we realized Nvidia had silently discontinued the feature. Our final approach, perhaps unsurprisingly, involved many large screenshots (taken with as much “pause” as the game would allow) stitched together into a equirectangular panoramic project using dedicated panorama domain-specific software (after both Adobe and open-source tools fared very poorly).

    When we first committed the nearly gigapixel-scale panoramas to the Hunt Git repository, we inadvertently discovered that this puzzle alone consumed ~52% of the repository’s disk space, despite already performing some JPG compression! For server-side technical reasons, we ended up having to downscale and fine-tune the compression on the image files. Hopefully the "zoom, enhance" was still viable enough for problem-solving purposes!

    We always planned to emulate GeoGuessr by providing an interactive panorama viewer - which we were able to implement in a very bare-bones manner in WebGL after reading through some open-source implementations. The presentation of the map, however, was only ever intended to be a straight, static image. However, every test-solve session we ran indicated that additional tooling would be super useful for either the accuracy of the extraction, or for the collaboration of remote team members, or both. So the map slowly morphed into a fairly Google Maps-style interactive interface, which kept eating up more and more development time. We obviously eventually shipped something, but we do sincerely apologize if you ran into any bugs!

    Hunt

    This was the first time either one of us wrote a puzzle for a hunt - hopefully it was enjoyable. And if anyone booted up their own copy of a game in order to work on their solve, it would make us super happy!
