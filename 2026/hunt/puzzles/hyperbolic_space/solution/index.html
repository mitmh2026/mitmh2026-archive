    Solution to Hyperbolic Space

    Answer: STROKES OF GENUS
    Written by: Dan Simon and Kevin Der
    Edited by: Benji Nguyen and Max Chang

    This solution will discuss both the round structure (which, even before the meta is unlocked, features backsolving), and the actual process of solving this metapuzzle once it is unlocked.

    Round Structure

    This round starts with 12 puzzles unlocked on the blue steps in round art, and four puzzles unlocked in red doors scattered throughout the page. The blue step puzzles are empty puzzles with just a title and the text "There doesn't seem to be anything here," while the red door puzzles are more normal and substantial. Solving the normal red door puzzles will unlock more red door puzzles; solving the empty blue step puzzles (though it is very unlikely to happen to a significant extent before making significant progress on the meta) will not unlock anything.

    After solving each puzzle in this round, whether normal or empty, we are shown (near the top of the puzzle page) an image of arrows in an outlined heptagon, and when hovering over the solved puzzle door/step, some other doors/steps will light up. This lighting-up is bidirectional; if puzzle A and puzzle B are both solved, and hovering A lights up B, hovering B will also light up A. Once we unlock all puzzles, we may also notice that hovering over each puzzle lights up seven other puzzles; three are always of the same type (normal/empty) and four are always of the other type. (Of course, without all puzzles unlocked, some of the normal puzzles that light up might not be unlocked and thus might not be visible yet.)

    Since all the puzzle answers are seven letters, it's natural to write the puzzle answers along the associated heptagon. A zip of the results of this is here. As before, an example, for Stray Child, is below. (This example uses magenta for the letters written in to make them distinguishable from the black lines.)

    Note that the first letter is always at the top, which weakly suggests that the heptagons can be rotated.

    The fact that hovering over each puzzle lights up seven others, and we have heptagons, may lead us to the conclusion that lighting up represents adjacency. Under this assumption, adjacent heptagons don't overlay nicely, but they can be put next to each other nicely. For example, we can put the heptagons with HOLDING (Had), MACHINE (Stray Child), and THUNDER (Combative Felines) next to each other around the H:

    The next key aha is that sometimes, it's possible to determine where an empty puzzle is in this structure. For example, the only two puzzles adjacent to both Stray Child and Combative Felines are Had, which is accounted for, and the empty puzzle Genealogy. Thus, we may realize that Genealogy is in the position above MACHINE/THUNDER on the diagram. This would seem to imply that the Genealogy answer has the letters E, N, and U. The word most appropriate to genealogy with these letters, assuming it's also seven letters like the normal puzzle answers are, is SURNAME, and that is in fact the correct answer to Genealogy.

    (It may be possible to guess 2 or 3 of the empty puzzle answers without understanding the meta by spamming title-related seven-letter guesses, but in testing this didn't seem strong enough to let teams break through. There is rate-limiting on guessing for the empty puzzles, though it is roughly 2x more lenient than on normal puzzles.)

    As mentioned above, when solving an empty puzzle, we also get an image of an outlined heptagon with arrows. The images of outlined heptagons for empty puzzles are zipped here.     Trying to use the adjacencies (as given by which puzzles light up) to make an overall diagram, we run into two issues. The first, which occurs even with just HOLDING/MACHINE/THUNDER, is that three regular heptagons don't fit around a vertex, hence the slight curvature of the lines in the image of those three heptagons above. The "Hyperbolic Space" theme and title of this round suggests the solution to this issue; the heptagons are in fact part of a heptagonal tiling of the hyperbolic plane.

    The second issue is that the diagram doesn't go on forever with more and more puzzles, as the hyperbolic plane does, but wraps around on itself. We can just recognize this wrapping and solve the puzzle anyway without knowing the exact details, but in fact the shape is the Klein quartic which we may find by looking up "24 heptagon tiling" or similar.

The blue and red match the color scheme on the website, both in the normal puzzles being on red doors while the empty puzzles are on blue steps, and in the puzzle list on the round page showing the puzzle titles in blue/red. We arbitrarily put HOLDING at the center; we could put any answer at the center and get a different representation of the same overall structure.

    Solving the Metapuzzle (Hyperbolic Space)

    When we solve 18 of the puzzles total (thus at least 6 of the empty puzzles), we unlock the actual metapuzzle. (Note that at this point, it is likely very easy to backsolve the rest of the puzzles and get all the data needed to construct the above shape.) This metapuzzle consists of 7 lines of 2 vague crossword-esque clues each with shapes and question marks. An example line (the first) is below:

    Clock parts? +
    		+ ?Apollo astronaut

    In the sixth of these seven lines (but in no others), the tetrahedral shapes have colors:

    Hardly rigid? +
    		+ ?NBA team

    The easiest break-in is that there are four triangles of all-mutually-adjacent blue (normal) puzzles and also four triangles of all-mutually-adjacent red (empty) puzzles, and each of these has a letter in the middle. These letters, as shown in the table below, are HILT for red and PRSS for blue; they are colored red and blue in the above diagram. Note that since there are four triangles for each color, each having three puzzles, each puzzle answer is used in exactly one of these triangles. Also note that it might seem that there are two red T's and two blue R's, but since these are on the edge, this is just because they each appear in two places in the diagram but are the same; the two blue S's, on the other hand, are not in the same triangle.

    It's possible to add an E to HILT to get LITHE and add a U to PRSS to get SPURS, which match the clues "Hardly rigid" and "NBA team." So, we probably extract this E and U.

    But what about the other lines? Those don't have colors to tell us what to extract, and it's unclear what vertices to use. It seems reasonable to keep the "four letters + one" idea, both since the other lines also have tetrahedra and since this would use each of the 56 letters in the diagram as part of one word. It turns out that due to symmetry of the Klein quartic, we can just look at the positions we got sets of four letters from and rotate one-seventh of a full turn to get new extraction positions; since the center and rotation of our above diagram was arbitrary, doing this gives us sets that are in a sense symmetrically equivalent to the original set.     Author's Notes

    Dan:

    Firstly, thanks to Kevin Der for writing the crossword clues. I did write an initial draft of them but the version you see is almost entirely Kevin's.

    The answer is of course a pun on the phrase STROKES OF GENIUS, while using the math term "genus" which is relevant to the hyperbolic geometry this meta involves. However, there is a somewhat forced justification for how it answers the flavortext's question "What do you need to fix this curved space?" It is a well-known result that the genus of a shape is the number of cuts you can make without disconnecting it, and it is also well-known the genus of the Klein quartic is 3. If you carefully follow the boundary between the normal puzzle heptagons and the empty puzzle heptagons, you will find that it makes four loops (each with 12 vertices/edges). Cutting along three of these loops (these cuts being the "strokes") produces a "fixed" genus-0 shape which is two spheres with three holes each, glued to each other along the uncut loop. Cutting along the last loop produces two spheres with four holes each, not connected to each other.

    Whether the answer refers to getting an unwieldy shape which is connected and genus-0 but contains both types of puzzles with three strokes (the genus), or getting two slightly more elegant/symmetric shapes (spheres with four holes) which are not connected, one with each type of puzzle, with four strokes (one more than the genus) is up to you. However, I'm writing this late enough that I'm fairly sure this is not going to be referenced in the story, so it doesn't matter.

    I think the specific tetrahedron/cube mechanism/cluing came from my reading about the Klein quartic and finding Greg Egan's page on the Klein quartic which many solvers will likely have found when searching combinations of "Klein quartic" and "cube"/"tetrahedron." The concept of using these cubes felt very natural to me. (The anticubes the page describes are not used; that would be extremely crazily constrained.) The tetrahedra that the meta uses aren't strictly speaking defined on that page (or anywhere as far as I know), but, just as a normal cube can be divided into two tetrahedra in a unique way, a cube from this meta has similar concepts of opposite and adjacent vertices and can similarly be divided into tetrahedra.

    The rotation described above is just one way (albeit the easiest way) to find the tetrahedra. If you start somewhere and then go along any of the three edges, turn right, go along that edge, turn left, and go along that edge, you will end up in a point in the tetrahedron paired to that of your original point (in fact, this is one way to define an adjacent vertex on the cube). This method can also be used to find the tetrahedra. However, it will often be confusing due to going off the edge.

    Based on a combination of early discussion about this meta and my vague memory, I think the puzzle came from a combination of:

    • A decision that every hard round would have something weird about it, and corresponding interest in finding at least some weird things that could accommodate normal puzzles (here, the puzzles with blank answers).
    • Other past rounds with blank answers (see here for a list of blank puzzles many of which are in such rounds), such as the MITMH 2018 Pokemon round (e.g. Persian) and the MITMH 2023 Wyrmhole round (specifically Collage, e.g. Bedtime Stories)
    • Metas involving the construction of some physical shape, combined with the fact that in past cases the shape has usually been two-dimensional or a polyhedron, rather than something hyperbolic like this.
    • More specifically, the 2024 MITMH Hell, Michigan round meta A Rift in Hades which also heavily featured the number 7 and a tessellation of a non-2D non-polyhedron (but also non-hyperbolic) shape by polygons (in this case, a genus-1 torus by hexagons).
    • The concept of a meta where you just make some structure rather than getting an answer, but you can still backsolve. (Obviously this meta does have an answer, but before using the actual meta, getting all 24 answers and filling out the full Klein quartic works like this; the meta is somewhat of a tacked-on extraction step.)
    • The page on the Klein quartic mentioned above

    Choosing answers for this meta was not too bad; hill-climbing basically worked. Maybe there is some answer set out there where words all read clockwise or counterclockwise, but if so I had and continue to have no idea how to find it efficiently.

    This meta required a few testsolves to get into a good shape. The meta data and answer set was there only a few days after the start of brainstorming (as far as I can tell from the channel, since my memory is not great), but various presentation changes took a long time to settle on. Various issues (e.g. testsolvers thinking the meta was about hyperspace rather than hyperbolic space, or overlaying heptagons rather than putting them next to each other, or thinking that they had added blue/red to their sheet rather than it being there initially) meant this took a while to get a fully clean testsolve, which was concerning, but in the end I think it ended up in a good state.

    I believe testsolves needed 7 to 9 answers to solve the meta. Early testsolves were on the higher end of that range, and when a testsolve was able to proceed with just 7 answers, it was a bit surprising. Fortunately, the late puzzles in this round are easy enough, and the geometry is unintuitive enough, that it's very possible at least one additional puzzle will be solved while the meta is still progressing to the point of backsolving the normal puzzles. I do think it's possible that some team will be guess-happy on the blank puzzles, get a few hits, and solve the round with only 5 forward solves. Partially due to concerns such as this, all normal puzzles in the round unlock with at most 6 solves (with width increasing from 4 to 6 normal puzzles as you solve normal puzzles).

    We considered an adjacency-based unlock system for this round, but we decided it wouldn't work well. It would either lead to large width (if the initial puzzles were far apart) or would allow too much potential of getting from the starting answers into the empty puzzles early with very small solve count (if the initial puzzles were close together). It also seemed that it would not give us enough control over unlock order.

    We had actually brainstormed this meta to some extent before ECPH ran, and were a bit worried when we came across the Tower round, which also used the concept of hyperbolic space. But we decided it was different enough from the concept here to allow us to proceed.

    The fact that LIGHT could also be taken as the answer to "Hardly rigid" (despite working less well than the intended LITHE) is somewhat unfortunate. It is very hard to write a clue for one but not the other, and we decided this was the best we could do. Hopefully any teams that fell into that were able to correct it eventually.

    This is probably one of my hardest puzzles in the hunt, and quite possibly my hardest. As someone who generally tries to avoid writing hard puzzles but does like math, it makes sense to me that it would happen in this way. I am actually not good at even 2-dimensional spatial reasoning, never mind what's going on here, so I had to make a lot of diagrams and write/trust a lot of code to be confident I was doing things correctly. I hope despite the math involved not being very well-known, it was accessible, and I hope teams all found some visualization solution that was strong enough to allow them to solve.
