    Solution to Replacements

    Answer: BRACED
    Written by: Dan Simon
    Edited by: Max Chang
    Canned responses
    GuessReply
    THE TECHKeep going!

    This puzzle consists of flavortext, four 4x4 KenKens labeled Puzzle 1-4, a line of text with letters and numbers (1a2 b3c4, with each letter/number underlined), and some mathematical expressions which refer to rows and columns of a grid. The mathematical expressions seem like extraction (or at least require filling out some grid), and it's unclear what to do with 1a2 b3c4, so the natural place to start is the KenKens.

    When we start to look at the KenKens, we may notice two issues. Firstly, each KenKen has one or two cages with a letter rather than a number, e.g. a×. These letters are a, b, and c, and we can probably guess that we have to determine the values of these letters from the rest of the clues.

    Secondly, more problematically, each KenKen has some clear impossibility. In most cases there is a clue that is impossible by itself (e.g., a sum that is far too high, or a product or quotient that is too high and/or divisible by 5). There is one puzzle (Puzzle 3) where all the clues seem valid, but there is one column consisting entirely of two 6+ clues, meaning the numbers in that column must add to 12 (rather than the usual 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10).

    We thus have to figure out how to change KenKen rules to be able to solve the KenKens. The title "Replacements" and flavor text "It's not as easy as 1-2-3-4." suggest replacing 1-2-3-4 somehow, and the natural way to do that is to use a different set of numbers (which we'll need to determine) for filling in each KenKen, rather than the usual 1, 2, 3, 4. (We could instead try to replace the clues, but the problematic clues are large, not in the 1-4 range, so this is not as clued by the flavortext.)

    Having made this assumption about using different numbers for filling in each KenKen, we still have to figure out what those numbers are. As it turns out, each KenKen is unique even only assuming the four numbers used in each are all integers, but the logical path is very complicated; to simplify, we'll make the additional assumption the four numbers used in each are positive. With the positivity assumption, a way to figure out each set of numbers is below. We could also assume the numbers in a row/column are distinct (i.e., there is not e.g. a constraint that each row has 1, 2, and two 3s), but this assumption does not simplify that much so we will not make it below; we will just assume that each row/column has the same multiset of four numbers.

    • Puzzle 1: 2 and 3 are given cells, so they must be two of the numbers. There is a 5+ in column 4; it must be either 2 + 3 or 1 + 4, but if it were 1 + 4, the 5÷ clues would be impossible. So the 5+ is 2 + 3. The 5÷ means the remaining two numbers apart from 2 and 3 are a factor of 5 apart. The 3 in column 1 must be part of the 60× or part of the 5÷. If part of the 60×, then 20 is used. If part of the 5÷, then 15 is used. The options for the numbers other than 2 and 3 are now 3 and 15, 15 and 75, 4 and 20, and 20 and 100. But there is also a 1- in row 1, which already has a 2. The only option where this is possible is 4 and 20 (where the 1- is 3 and 4). Thus, the numbers used are 2, 3, 4, 20.
    • Puzzle 2: 1 is a given cell so must be one of the numbers. Column 1 consists of the 1 and a 60×, so the product of all four numbers must be 60. There is a two-cell 12×, so the product of the two numbers in it is 12 and the product of the other two numbers is 5. The only way to get 5 is 1 × 5, so 5 is also one of the numbers. There is a 4+ which must be 2 and 2 or 1 and 3. If the 4+ is 2 and 2, then the product is not 60, so the 4+ must be 1 and 3. The product of the three known numbers 1, 3, and 5 is 15, so to get the product of 60 the fourth number must be 4. So the numbers used are 1, 3, 4, 5.
    • Puzzle 3: 1 and 2 are given cells, so they must be two of the numbers. As noted above in the solution, there is one column (the fourth column) consisting entirely of two 6+ clues. This not only means the numbers in that column must add to 12, but also means that (since they only add to 3) the 1 and 2 must be split between the 6+ clues, which must be 1 + 5 and 2 + 4 in some order. So the numbers used are 1, 2, 4, 5.
    • Puzzle 4: This puzzle has no given cells, but it does have a ((rotated) L-shaped) 3-cell 4+, which must be 2 and two 1s. (Cages are allowed to repeat numbers as long as there is no repetition in a row or column. Of course, we're here assuming repetition might be allowed anyway as long as every row/column has repetition.) This means 1 and 2 are two of the four numbers. The first two columns are made of a 24×, a 4+, and a 13+, which adds to 17 plus the sum of the two numbers in the 24×. But these two columns also sum to twice the sum of the four numbers, and thus sum to something even. So the sum of the two numbers in the 24× is odd, and this makes it easy to see that it is either 1 × 24 or 3 × 8. If it is 1 × 24, the 24× has one of the two 24s in the first or second column, but the other 24 in the first or second column can be in neither the 4+ or the 13+, so cannot be anywhere, leading to a contradiction. So it is 3 × 8. So the numbers used are 1, 2, 3, 8.

    Of course, making additional assumptions makes the above logic easier. We've discussed assuming numbers in a row/column are distinct, but also, only one of 1-4 is replaced in each grid, and the one that is replaced is the number of the puzzle (e.g., in Puzzle 1, 1 is replaced with 20, meaning the numbers used are 2, 3, 4, 20 rather than 1, 2, 3, 4). We aren't expected to figure this out immediately, but after finding the number set used for and/or solving one or two of the puzzles, it's likely to become clear.

    After determining the four numbers in each grid, we should not have much difficulty solving the grids. Given the four numbers, it basically suffices to determine the options for each region individually and eliminate options that conflict with already-placed cells. Of course, assuming that each of a, b, and c are the same in their appearances (two each, in different puzzles: a appears in Puzzles 1 and 2, b appears in Puzzles 2 and 3, and c appears in Puzzles 3 and 4) also helps.

    Skippable note: The most complicated logic for these four puzzles is that the four two-cell regions with non-letter clues in Puzzle 2 each have a known pair of numbers that (whichever way around it is) fixes which way around another of the two-cell regions is. The 12× being 3 and 4 means R3C2 in the 4+ is 1, the 4+ being 1 and 3 means R3C4 in the 6+ is 5, the 6+ being 1 and 5 means R2C4 in the 3- is 4, and the 3- being 1 and 4 means R2C2 in the 12× is 3. But this is common KenKen logic, and in all three other puzzles, one can in fact figure out which way around each two-cell region is before using it to eliminate options.

    At this point, we have solved the KenKens and used the flavortext, but we still have the 1a2 b3c4 and the mathematical expressions. The expressions have the issue of referring to rows and columns up to 6, e.g. the first one is '7 + R2C4 + R6C4' which refers to a nonexistent row 6. We could try to combine the grids into an 8-by-8 grid, but then it becomes surprising that rows/columns 7 and 8 are not used. In fact, the next place to look is not the expressions, but instead the 1a2 b3c4 line.

    From getting the KenKen solutions, we can see that a = 8, b = 20, and c = 3. As for 1, 2, 3, and 4, we haven't yet used the replacements for 1, 2, 3, and 4 in the associated KenKens. 1 was replaced with 20, 2 with 5, 3 also with 5, and 4 with 8. We can thus make the following table.     This means that 1a2 b3c4 = THE TECH. This is a partial that, if we put it in the answer checker, will tell us "Keep going!" The Tech is MIT's weekly student newspaper (as we can find by searching if we don't already know). The Tech is available both online (with an archive of past issues) and physically on-campus, so it is very easy to find a copy of it. Most issues of The Tech have a KenKen; specifically, the most recent issue of The Tech at time of hunt has the following KenKen:

    This gives the message REDO AS PUZZLE SIX. The meaning of this is to redo the KenKen from The Tech, but this time as Puzzle 6. Since in the previous part of the puzzle, x was replaced by another number in Puzzle x, this means that here, 6 should be replaced by another number, for us to determine.

    The first step is to find what number replaces 6. Since there is a 18× clue with two numbers, which is impossible with just 1 through 5 regardless of constraints on repetition, the number that 6 is replaced by must be a factor of 18, and it must be at least 6. The options are 6, 9, and 18 itself. 6 obviously cannot be replaced by 6, since that would give the same solution. If 6 is replaced by 9, there is a 6÷ clue that it is impossible to satisfy. So 6 is replaced by 18.

    Knowing that the numbers used are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 18, the KenKen is solvable. Online solvers are not generally able to handle different sets of used numbers and the solution with these numbers is of course not in The Tech, so (unless we find some workaround such as writing our own solver) we will need to solve this KenKen manually, which is comparable in difficulty to solving the KenKen manually with the standard number set of 1-6.     Author's Notes

    Thanks to The Tech for allowing us to embed a KenKen in their puzzle section.

    When we wanted to have a puzzle involving using The Tech puzzle section, we realized it needed to be trivialized neither by online solvers nor by the solutions being given. So, it needed to have some twist(s) making online solvers not work, while still working normally so that The Tech readers not doing MIT Mystery Hunt could still solve it. With that, it was natural to have a step where the puzzle needed to be solved again.

    Testsolvers generally considered the fact that the puzzle in The Tech could be solved twice to be impressive; as the constructor, I don't think it was that bad. Admittedly, it involved a lot of brute-force search for a puzzle that could be solved twice in this way, but the search was not that complicated, and the two solutions are (somewhat inelegantly) fairly similar (only 14 of the 36 cells are the same between them, but a lot of others are just swapped pairs of adjacent cells). Once I had both solutions, it was also fairly easy to find equations that gave the letters I needed for both cluephrases (REDO AS PUZZLE SIX and LOOK AT BOTTOM ROW).

    Testsolves had more difficulty than I expected with some steps, especially those surrounding THE TECH and PUZZLE SIX (the earlier form of REDO AS PUZZLE SIX). For THE TECH, it was unclear that each of 1a2 b3c4 corresponded with a letter, meaning testsolvers took some time to get THE TECH. (The underscores were added to hopefully make this step slightly clearer/easier.) Even after getting it, it was sometimes not obvious what it meant to testsolvers without an MIT connection. (Of course, this was also somewhat hard to test because the most recent The Tech at time of testsolving did not have the KenKen used in this puzzle.) As for PUZZLE SIX, it was unclear that the 6-by-6 KenKen needed to be re-solved with just that information; REDO AS was added to assist with this.

    It's theoretically possible to short-circuit the last step by either (earlier in the solve of "Puzzle 6") noticing the set of numbers used, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 18, anagrams to BRACED under A=1 Z=26, or (somewhat later) noticing the bottom row (one of the earlier areas to be filled out in the solve) spells BRACED under A=1 Z=26. However, no testsolvers actually did that; it's hard if you're not looking for it.

    This puzzle originally had a very different form, where the KenKens were too hard, too numerous, and (being standard) too vulnerable to online solvers. This earlier version is presented below.

    Note that the answer was different, so if you are e.g. postsolving this hunt, you should probably not look. Testsolvers who solved both versions disagreed on which was preferable (at least, ignoring the difficulty and online solver considerations).
