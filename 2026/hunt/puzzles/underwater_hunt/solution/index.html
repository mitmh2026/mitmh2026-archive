    Solution to Underwater Hunt

    Answer: NOVA SALMON
    Written by: Kevin Der and Dan Simon
    Edited by: Benji Nguyen
    Additional thanks to:
    • Alison Chang for making images for several levels

    Puzzle Structure

    This puzzle consists of multiple levels (though due to the round's adjacency unlocks, we will only be able to access a few of them when first unlocking it). The levels have in common:

    • The title "Underwater Hunt,"
    • The flavortext "You remember your first week at MIT, when you took a field trip to study the underwater world, a yearly tradition since 2012. To explore there once again, you'll need to remember the past... but sea life has also gotten more complicated since then. When you learn something, send us the original year and the new answer:"
    • An answer checker, as mentioned in the flavortext

    Searching phrases from the flavortext will hopefully lead us to the MIT Aquarium Puzzle Hunt, a yearly hunt at MIT. If we've heard of or done this hunt before, we will likely also remember it without searching. This hunt matches the description in the flavortext. Specifically, the "Aquarium" name and the fact that it is run at the New England Aquarium in most years fits well with the mentions of "underwater" in the title and flavortext. To further confirm, the Aquarium Hunt is run on the last day of orientation week, it is for first-year undergraduates, and it started in 2012.

    The levels, in addition to looking fairly distinct from each other in terms of tile sets, also each have their own title and flavortext. The global puzzle flavortext saying "you'll need to remember the past..." may cause us to look at past Aquarium Hunts via the archive (at the same link above). Looking through the archive, we may notice that the title and flavortext for each individual level suggests one of the past Aquarium Hunt themes, so it is natural to try to match them. Furthermore, for each level/hunt pair, through a combination of level flavortext and level appearance, we can then match the level to a specific puzzle from that hunt. Since there are only roughly 10 puzzles per hunt, often all in a single PDF, it is not too bad to look through them all to find the match (though it does add up over the course of 12 levels).

    Having matched the levels to specific Aquarium Hunt puzzles, we still need to solve the levels. As previously mentioned, "you'll need to remember the past..." suggests that we want to use the original puzzle, but "sea life has also gotten more complicated since then" (also from the flavortext) suggests that there might be some new puzzle mechanic not in the original puzzle, which indeed there is. Of course, there is the fact that this is the tile round so we are placing tiles in each level, but even separately from that, every level has some sort of change to how the original puzzle works.

    We also have to keep in mind a few additional things. The presence of an answer checker which takes both a year and answer suggests that each level (naturally associated with a year of Aquarium Hunt) has its own answer, in addition to having a tile placement. We may not realize this at first, since the answers are sometimes not obvious, but hopefully eventually the answer checker and unused data in certain levels should alert us to it.

    Also, in most years (the main exception being 2025, which does have solutions), Aquarium Hunt has no solutions online, so we may have to also solve the original puzzles. We don't strictly need to solve the original puzzle, but usually we need to at least understand something about how it works to place the tiles, get the level answer, or both (though there are some exceptions, mostly where the original puzzle mechanism being used is intuitive enough that we can rederive it). If we are lucky, e.g. helped to write Aquarium Hunt in the past or (more likely) solved some past Aquarium Hunts ourselves, we may have access to (some) solutions, and a few solutions are online publicly in various places. It sometimes also helps to use the fact that you can find the original puzzle answers (though not full solutions) in the source code of the archived answer checker; this doesn't reveal the puzzle mechanism directly, but knowing the answer can sometimes help inspire ideas.

    Individual Levels

    The levels are given in alphabetical order by title, for convenience in finding them in the solution.

    Accordion

    This level's flavortext is "To save the penguins, Galileo has turned to music-making." This matches the 2012 Aquarium Hunt, which according to the archive page "asked teams to reverse an apocalypse with the help of the Penguin Federation." As expected, looking at the hunt story will confirm this match.

    To match with a specific puzzle, the mention of Galileo should lead us to Puzzle 3 – Telescope, which doesn't look very similar to this puzzle but does mention Galileo in the flavortext.

    The original puzzle works by starting with a nine-letter word at the top and removing one letter in each row while still having a word after each removal, creating an upside-down triangle that looks like a telescope. Once we realize this is the mechanism, we may know (or may search) the standard factoid that there is only one nine-letter word that can give a one-letter word this way. The diagram also shows us some given letters, and where the removals are. (The factoid is actually false; there are other such words. However, the given letters and removal locations specify the nine-letter word anyway.) However we find it, the answer (the original nine-letter word) is STARTLING (or at least, we can reasonably presume it's the answer; the 2012 archive doesn't have an answer checker).

    This level has a different visual presentation, with minus signs below some letters and plus signs above others. This suggests that between every two rows, rather than just removing a letter, we remove the letter with a minus below it in the upper row and add the letter with a plus above it in the lower row. Each row is 5 letters, which is consistent with both removing and adding a letter between two rows. The locations of minus and plus signs also match (one minus sign in each row but the bottom row, one plus sign in each row but the top row). Finally, this idea matches with the general shape of an accordion; rather than getting thinner like a telescope, it contracts and expands as you go down its length.

    Now that we've figured out how the puzzle works, we need to solve it. We are not given any letters directly, but we do have a tile bank of exactly enough letters to fill every tile. Assuming that as in the original puzzle, the order of the letters that are not removed or added between two words stays the same, we know certain letters propagate downwards. For example, the second letter of the first word ends up also being the second letter of the fifth word. This gives us numbers of occurrences for various letters, which we can try to match up with our tile bank. We can also guess and check; our tile bank has only 7 distinct letters (ACLOSTU), and there aren't that many reasonably common five-letter words we can make with those letters. We can also try to run a programmatic search. Whatever method we take, we will find that the only possible solution with the given tile bank is LOCUS → LOUTS → CLOTS → COATS → COAST:

    Neither the top nor the bottom word is the answer, but the letters on the eight cells with plus and minus signs, in grid cell reading order (left-to-right, top-to-bottom), spell the answer, CUT CLASS.

    Always Be Changing

    This level's flavortext is "Dear Inspector, to save the school mascot you'll need to study advanced organisms, not just rocket science ... (7)" This matches the 2016 Aquarium Hunt, which according to the archive page "asked teams to play 20 Questions to save Tim the Beaver." (Tim the Beaver is the MIT school mascot.) Note that the 2025 Aquarium Hunt "asked teams to investigate why Tim was absent from Orientation.", which is somewhat similar, but looking at both we see that the flavortext for each puzzle in the 2016 Aquarium Hunt starts with "Dear Inspector," making it a better match. The specific puzzle this level corresponds to is 9 – Atomic Bonded Compound, which mentions "rocket scientists" in flavor, has the same ABC title acronym, and (less distinctively) has clues with word answers. (The (7) in the level flavortext is used later as will be described, and is not for puzzle matching.)

    The original puzzle works by giving clues for two words and combining them to make a Pokémon name, sometimes dropping parts of one or both words. Sometimes this is fairly straightforward, e.g. TOTO + DIALS = TOTODILE. Sometimes it's more stretched, e.g. SQUIRLE + TULSA = SQUIRTLE or VAPOR + RIHANNA = VAPOREON. In the original puzzle, each Pokémon name is 8 letters and one letter of each is extracted by given dots connected by dashed lines, with the first and last in shaded cells. Reading the extracted letters gives HIJKLMNO. This is similar to the ABC title acronym, but the title more directly hints at chemistry; these letters should be interpreted as H2O (H to O), which is WATER.

Some of the tile clues have numbers at the end, but we ignore those for now; we may initially think these numbers are answer lengths, but for some clues such as "Golden ratio symbol (4)," there is a clear answer (here PHI) that has a different length. Some tiles are pre-placed while some are not. There are also some thick black lines horizontally separating adjacent tiles, breaking rows up into groups of 2 or 3.     Collection of Pearls

    The flavortext for this level refers to "befriending cartoon characters" which corresponds to the 2022 Aquarium Hunt, and in particular the puzzle 6 – Saturday Morning Showings.

    The original puzzle gives a number of crossword-style clues whose answers are all 7 letters long. Furthermore, an image of the television test pattern called SMPTE color bars is given. Each 7 letter answer is associated with one of those particular colors. Imagining that the answer is written out one letter per color bar, take the letter corresponding to the matching color for each clue, and concatenate those letters to get the answer.

    This puzzle is similar in that it also has crossword clues albeit in a hexagonal grid, and each slot has a particular background color. The answers to these clues are quite straightforward, allowing the level's tiles to be placed correctly fairly easily. Note that the clue answers do not always go all the way to the end of the line they are on, though they do sometimes intersect.

    The solver may be interested in where these colors might come from. The flavortext mentions "wax pencils" and the level title is "Collection of Pearls" — this suggests Crayola crayons, and indeed there is a set of 24 colors called Pearl Crayons that exactly matches the colors given. Each of these colors has a descriptive name. As in the original puzzle, each clue answer is associated with one particular color, in this case semantically. For example, the answer METEOR corresponds with the color called Shooting Star. Just as before, note which letter of the answer is associated with the particular corresponding color, for each entry. Reading these letters in grid order gives the answer DARTERS. The crossword answers, colors, and extracted letters are given below:

    Dangerous Waters

    This level's flavortext refers to "institutions of so-called higher learning" which matches the 2021 Aquarium Hunt, and the level title matches 5 – Sharkmouth University.

    The original puzzle is a straightforward hashi-like logic puzzle where bridges are drawn between islands and the total length of bridges connected to a particular island must match the given number on the island. In addition, bridges may not touch whirlpools or cross each other.

    In this puzzle, the exact same mechanic is used, with the difference that some bridges can turn at 120 degree angles. One starting point in the logic is noticing that very few places allow the "3 o'clock and 7 o'clock" tile to be placed there. Furthermore, the tile southwest of the given whirlpool only has two options, one of which is correct and the other leads to failure of the "<3" constraint.

    To extract, take the sum of bridge lengths for each island and convert to letters using A1Z26 to get the answer FADE.

    Imaginary Architecture

    This level's flavortext is "In dreams, the corridors of your mind can lead to unexpected places." This matches the 2015 Aquarium Hunt, which according to the archive page "asked teams to save MIT's scholarship fund, Inception-style." (Inception is a movie about dreams, and the hunt story also mentions dreams.) The specific puzzle is 7 – Shady Labyrinth, which is also a maze, and which mentions "architecture" in flavor. (The meta for 2015 is also a maze, but is not as architecture-themed).

    The original puzzle is fairly simple. We solve the maze (which in fact is a labyrinth; from the start, there's only one path to the end, with no dead-ends or forks), and look at the non-black-rectangle regions that are not in the solution. (These non-solution regions do have some dead ends and forks.) Looking at the non-solution regions, we can see they spell 6.28, giving the answer TAU.

    In this level, we need to piece together the maze. Each tile is a piece of the maze, and we need to fit them together so that they match along edges. The key realization here is that the length of the perimeter of the level is the same as the number of un-crossed hexagon sides, so making the reasonable assumption that no edges go to the perimeter of the level, we know every un-crossed hexagon side is on the perimeter. This lets us place roughly half of the perimeter immediately and reduces options for the other half. Either logic or trial and error will lead us to the unique way to fit the tiles together:

    We still need to solve this maze. Starting at the start arrow, we can follow a single path without forks as in the original puzzle. However, we will eventually hit a blue circle at a dead end. We need to have the realization that the circles are portals; we can jump from a circle to another circle of the same color. This allows us to go from the other blue circle to a red circle, from the other red circle to a green circle, and then finally from the other green circle to the end. As before, we notice some unvisited areas. (And as in the original puzzle, the unvisited areas do have some "true" dead ends and have one fork.) Here is an image where the maze path is drawn in black and the unvisited areas are drawn in red:

    The unvisited areas spell √(-1), giving us the answer i (thematic given the "imaginary" in flavortext).

    Lost Princess

    This level's flavortext is "Parts of the kingdom are now overlapping, but otherwise all the standard rules apply." This matches the 2023 Aquarium Hunt. This is not as clear from the archive page, which says that this hunt "asked teams to help Ariel's friends think of a gift to give her," but clicking through to the actual hunt story we see each puzzle was themed around a Disney princess. The specific puzzle is 3 – Elsa (link goes to PDF with all puzzles; scroll down), which has similar thermometers and has "lost" in the flavortext.

    In the original puzzle, solve the sudoku using the rules at the bottom, which are standard rules for thermo sudoku. The shaded squares and circles are, as the puzzle says, for extraction; the original flavortext's "What should she dial in her phone to get help?" clues using multitap phone code, which turns 3 (x3), 4 (x3), 7 (x3), 3, 9, 6 (x3), 7 (x3), 5 (x2) into the answer FIREWORK. (There is actually an erratum in the original puzzle; the fourth letter 3 should be 3 (x2) to give E. This does not affect this puzzle.)

    In this level, we solve a similar thermo sudoku. However, there are red, green, and blue outlines around certain 9-cell areas. This is what "Parts of the kingdom are now overlapping" refers to; these 9-cell areas take the role of boxes in a standard square sudoku, in that they each need to use each digit 1-9 exactly once. Also, the rule that no two numbers can be the same in a row or column now applies in three directions, since the puzzle is on a hexagonal grid. Apart from these changes, as the flavortext says, the rules are the same.

    The logic to get the solution is surprisingly complicated given the small puzzle size. Here's a description using the following cell naming system:

    Firstly, RB1 and R1 have to be less than 5 by thermometer rules. But if RG is 5, then all of RB3, R2, and R4 are also less than 5, creating 5 different cells in the red region less than 5. So RG is not 5. But R1, being less than 5, is also not 5. R2, being on a diagonal with B3 (the given 5), is also not 5. And R3, being a given 9, is also not 5. The only remaining cell in red that is not also in blue is R4, and since blue already has its given 5, R4 must be 5. This forces R2 = 6, RB3 = 7, RG = 8, and G4 = 9.

    RB1-3 are in the same row as RG and G4-6, and in blue with RGB and GB, so they must all be in G1-3. This means that RB1-3 are G1-3, but potentially in a different order. The largest of RB1-3 is the 7 in RB3, since RG and R3 use up 8 and 9. The largest of G1-3 is G1, since G1 is at the end of the thermometer. So G1 = 7. But now G2 is fairly constrained. It must be in the 4-6 range due to thermometer constraints, but RB1-3 can't be 5-6 because of R2 and R4. So G2 = 4 and G6 = 3. At this point the logic starts to get easier; we can fill in the rest of the green region by eliminating options, and then fill in the red and blue regions similarly (using thermometer rules a tiny bit to determine which of RB1-2 is which of 2 and 4). We get this solution:

    Unlike in the original puzzle, no cells are shaded. But some cells do have circled numbers which we interpret as multitap phone code, as in the original puzzle. 3 (x3), 8 (x2), 6 (x2), 5 (x2) gives us the answer FUNK.

    (Interesting fact: There is logic to deduce that R3 = G4 and then that R4 = 5 (and so R3 = G4 = 9) even without the given 9. To put it another way, the given 9 is redundant. However, this version of the puzzle seemed too hard.)

    No Droids Allowed

    This level's flavortext is "To verify their humanity, these cultural exchange students must pass a more difficult test by making some adjustments, but the way they'll read it won't change. It'll help to count to 7, 6, 3, and 5, and discard everything else." Much of this flavortext will be used for the puzzle directly and is not hinting at a specific Aquarium Hunt year or puzzle, but we can tell that this matches the 2019 Aquarium Hunt, which according to the archive page "asked teams to solve some puzzles written through a cultural scholarship program." The specific puzzle is 1 – I'm Not a Robot which has the same CAPTCHA theming and also has grids of images.

    In the original puzzle, we just find the images matching the prompts (bow, chip, check, bar) in each 3x3 grid. The cells used by these images in each 3x3 grid make the shapes of the letters T, I, L, T, giving the answer TILT.

    In this level, each tile has two images, we're not given a list of words to try matching against, the puzzle isn't split into subgrids, and some of the tiles aren't placed. The first natural thing to do is to try to identify the images. We should hopefully eventually notice, in identifying the images, that the names of many of them are similar to each other, and in fact are similar to the words BOW, CHIP, CHECK, and BAR from the original puzzle. For example, the given tiles have a boa (constructor), a Bob (Odenkirk), a speech bubble coming from a ghost (cluing "boo"), a (ro)bot, and a car being towed (cluing "tow"). The tiles to place include a cardboard box and a cow. All of BOA, BOB, BOO, BOT, TOW, BOX, and COW are one letter away from BOW.

    This one-letter change is also hinted by "making some adjustments" in the flavortext. It's also potentially helpful that some of the non-matching images in the original puzzle, i.e. a clip, ship, whip for CHIP and a car for BAR are re-used as one-letter-off images in this puzzle, though different images of the relevant things are chosen (e.g., not the same ship image).

    The natural thing to do is then figure out which images in the given grid and in the tile bank are one letter off from the words BOW/CHIP/CHECK/BAR. Here's a table with this data:

    WordOne-letter-off words in gridOne-letter-off words in tile bank
    BOWBOA, BOB, BOO, BOT, TOWBOX, COW
    CHIPCHIA, CHIN, SHIPCHOP, CLIP, WHIP
    CHECKCHEEK, CHICKCHUCK
    BARBAT, EAR, JARBAG, CAR

    Conveniently, the total number of images one letter off of each word in the grid and bank combined matches the numbers "7, 6, 3, and 5" given in the flavortext, which confirms we didn't miss one and suggests this is the right thing to do.

    Note that this uses 21 total images. There are 19 tiles, each with 2 images, so 17 images are unused. There are 4 tiles where both images are used, and 2 where neither image is used. Solvers might expect exactly one image to be used from each tile, but in fact this can be disproven by realizing the 7, 6, 3, and 5 are counts and then doing the above arithmetic to see too many images are used for that to happen, or by finding one of the tiles that is a counterexample.

    The flavortext saying "discard everything else" suggests we ignore the other 17 so-far-unused images. "the way they'll read it won't change" suggests, as in the original puzzle, that once we place all the tiles, we should just look at the tiles with each type of one-letter-off image and try to interpret the shape they form as a letter.

    But how do we place the tiles? The main constraint is actually that the shape of each of the four groups of tiles must be a letter shape. A good place to start are the BOW tiles, which are fairly clearly forming a V (and it's clear where the two missing slots are). However, even doing this, we have two tiles with BRICK/CHOP and CLIP/DOVE, which both have one word in the CHIP group and one red herring, and there seems to be no way to determine which goes where.

    For this, we use the fact that within each group, alphabetical order is reading order. Though this is not clued, there is enough data in the given tiles to determine it (e.g., the chance of BOA/BOB/BOO/BOT/TOW coincidentally having the same reading order and alphabetical order is fairly low). This alphabetical-order observation may also help us resolve letter shape ambiguities, since it makes some tile placements impossible. (Note that for any single tile, the image in the top-left comes alphabetically before the image in the bottom-right. Hopefully this reminds us of alphabetical order and helps us have the "alphabetical order is reading order" realization. Note also that if we have the correct letter shapes, the BRICK/CHOP and CLIP/DOVE tiles will be the only ambiguous tiles, so if we are missing this idea we can just try both options.)

    The solution is shown in the image below:

    Here are the groups, drawn with blue/green/orange/purple lines for BOW/CHIP/CHECK/BAR respectively. We connect centers of tiles; if we connect actual image locations instead, the result is similar but the lines are more wiggly. If we shade tiles (perhaps the closest thing to an actual CAPTCHA, where one selects tiles rather than drawing lines between them), the results are again similar. Note that the choice of lines for the purple group (d), in particular the lack of a line between BAT/HIVE and BAR/WED, is motivated by trying to get some letter from it.

    Reading in BOW/CHIP/CHECK/BAR order (the same order as the original puzzle and as the given numbers in the flavortext), we can see that the answer is VOID.

    Ruler of the Night Sky

    The title and flavortext suggest the puzzle 4 – Moonking in the 2018 Aquarium Hunt.

    The original puzzle is a wordsearch where star symbols in the grid are read as the letters STAR. In this puzzle, one can think of the solver as placing tiles to create a grid where a wordsearch can take place. There are also tiles with an emoji that depicts stars on it, and each such tile has a tooltip containing a crossword clue. When all tiles are correctly placed, the answer to each emoji tile's crossword clue can be found in the grid intersecting that tile; however, instead of the letters STAR, the rebus for the emoji tile is instead the name of one of the 88 IAU designated constellations. To extract, follow the original puzzle and take the letters not used by the wordsearch; in this case those letters are MAPS, the answer.     TechCASH Windfall

    This level's flavortext is "Doing well on quiz shows can be very lucrative." This matches the 2017 Aquarium Hunt, which according to the archive page "asked teams to play the spiciest Friday night activity of Jeopardy." The specific puzzle is 8 – Selective Winning which mentions "techcash" in its flavortext.

    In the original puzzle, we have a list of techcash amounts that are powers of 2, in four columns of five. (The columns are column 1: 1-16, column 2: 32-512, column 3: 1024-16384, and column 4: 32768-524288.) We are also given the text "Net: TCH 272285." The original puzzle's flavortext "You win some, you lose some. It's a binary choice." suggests adding some of these powers of two and subtracting others to get 272285, with each being either added or subtracted. A table of which powers of two we add and subtract in the original puzzle is:

    -1-32-1024-32768
    +2+64-2048-65536
    +4+128+4096+131072
    +8+256-8192-262144
    -16+512-16384+524288

    Taking the + as a binary 1 and the - as a binary 0, and reading down each column, we get the answer NODE.

    In this level, we aren't given any net amount; we're given a grid with number clues pointing along lines in three directions (with 2-6 cells per clue line), and with three pre-placed numerical tiles. The numbers on our tiles are bigger than the numbers in the original puzzle; for example, one of the pre-placed tiles is 1,162,261,467. After some searching, we should realize this level uses the first 20 powers of 3 once each, rather than the first 20 powers of 2.

    It may not initially be clear what the number clues do. However, many of the number clues are divisible by large powers of 3. More generally, the number clues often have many zeros or twos when written in ternary. Also, the clues on lines with the pre-placed 1,162,261,467, which is the biggest tile value (319), are the biggest clues, and are relatively close to 1,162,261,467. From these facts, or just from analogizing from the original puzzle, we should hopefully conclude that the clues are obtained from the values on their line by adding some and subtracting others, just as in the original puzzle.

    This is slightly more complicated than adding and subtracting powers of two in the original puzzle, because we don't know which powers of three to use, and also the numbers involved are bigger making arithmetic harder. However, since we can't use any tile more than once along a line, finding which tiles make a clue is equivalent to writing the clue value in balanced ternary, a version of ternary where the digits are -1 (often written as T for clarity), 0, and 1. Indeed, if we write a clue value in balanced ternary (for example, 7,217 = 1010T010T), we can add the place values with 1 (here, 38 = 6,561, 36 = 729, and 32 = 9) and subtract the place values with T (here, 34 = 81 and 30 = 1) to get the clue value (6,561 + 729 + 9 - 81 - 1 = 7,217), and this is the only way to get it without reuse. Balanced ternary converters can be found online, and it is also fairly easy to program one. The number of cells along each clue line is of course the number of nonzero digits in the clue value's balanced ternary representation, which helps to confirm we understand what to do correctly and also helps to catch arithmetic errors.

    This tells us which powers of 3 are on each line and if they're positive or negative, but not what order to put them in. For ordering, we use the fact that every cell is on a line in at least two different directions. (Most cells are on a line in three directions, but some cells are on a line of length one in one of the three directions, e.g. there is neither a cell to their left or to their right. Those cells are unclued in that direction, since the clue would just be the cell value if it existed.) Specifically, we can use the fact that for any two lines, their intersection value must be the single place value that is nonzero in both corresponding clue values' balanced ternary representations. It will turn out that all the clues crossing a number give it the same sign (either it's added in all of them, or subtracted in all of them), which is useful additional confirmation.
