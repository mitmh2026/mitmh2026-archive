    Solution to Novelty Store

    Answer: LOOPER

    TL;DR - This puzzle is inspired by Tamagotchi and Pokémon Go, and involves playing a series of games to build rapport and understanding with the TaMITgotchi version of the child, to learn its dance alphabet language and earn a final cluephrase.

    Intro: Pick Up & Hatching

    Upon unlocking the puzzle, solvers are told to go pick something up from the "Novelty Store". They are given the TaMITgotchi in an egg-shaped case. The screen shows an egg with a progress bar.

    Solvers will need to walk a set distance to hatch the TaMITgotchi.

    Main puzzle: Playing games to fulfill TaMITgotchi needs

    Every 2 minutes, the TaMITgotchi beeps to signal that it needs something. The solver must choose and play the corresponding game to "fulfill" this need.

    Each time the solver completes the game successfully, they increase their accomplishment level within that category. Once the solver has succeeded in a certain category 4 times, they reach the highest "achievement". The TaMITgotchi performs a happy dance accompanied by beeping a relevant word in Morse code.

    Success condition: The solver must complete at least 10 lifts.    		EXERCISE

    Finale

    After solvers reach final achievements in all 5 categories, the locked category (the third icon on the menu) will unlock. Selecting this menu item will show the TaMITgotchi performing a longer happy dance.

    Solvers should recognize the dance moves from the category-specific accomplishment dances. Specifically, each of those 5 dances had the same number of dance moves as letters in the Morse word, allowing solvers to create a mapping between the alphabet and dance moves.

    Authors' Notes

    "I don't necessarily recommend spinning up a small scale consumer electronics startup... and definitely don't do it while writing a mystery hunt". - D&M at MITMH 2025 Wrap Up

    If you're here because you want to still care for your TaMITgotchi, you can find documentation and care instructions located on the Github page for the project linked here: https://github.com/mitmh2026/taMITgotchi.

    Beginnings

    After the hunt theme was selected and we knew we were going to pursue the research task concept, teammates wanted to see some sort of pedometer concept as a potential research task (to encourage physical activities i.e. touching grass during hunt). There are a good number of team members that are fans of the Pokémon Go game. If step tracking was the only thing we were interested in, the thought was perhaps to hand teams pedometers in bulk rather than designing something ourselves. However, there was an appetite to build something perhaps that could be hatched a la Pokémon Go, and eggs also felt thematic with this concept as well. At the same time, the concept of Tamagotchis were brought up as well, and a custom “Tamagotchi” that could serve as both a pedometer and a game device seemed worth building. It was decided that we could fit at least 1 puzzle in the device as well as a few research tasks. During the June retreat, we co-parented a real Tamagotchi to understand some of the appeal and how they generally worked. We also had to make a few calls at this retreat to keep the scope small for this device:

    • No wireless connectivity: We wanted to keep it true to the original Tamagotchi and not over-complicate it, e.g. syncing data to servers etc.
    • No battery charging: Having seen firsthand what can go wrong with that (IYKYK about our radio), we wanted this small device to last the entirety of Hunt without charging it. Also there was a good chance these would be assembled on the west coast and air transport of typical rechargeable Li-ion batteries have much stricter rules than alkaline batteries.

    After this ideation, we were off to the races!

    Initial Development

    Because this effort wasn't started in earnest until shortly before the June retreat, we needed to split the workstreams to get this done by our fall retreat in late September. To do this we split the initial work between Art Development / Game Development and Prototype 1 (Proto1) Hardware Development.

    Art and Game Development

    We quickly selected a style of I2C OLED screen off Amazon as our target screen for the device because it was easy to source and had a lot of community support for it. We then built 2 Arduino Uno rigs with this screen attached. These two devices served to get the initial game development and the art for the game.

    Shortly into this development, we realized that our screen was actually quite high in resolution, and the Atmel chips in typical Arduinos were not going to cut it. There would be real storage pressure in the flash storage. During this time before the fall retreat we were able to get the first two games working, the menu navigation, and the dances for the achievements and the final extraction done. This initial development had to be done in chunks though because all the assets didn't fit on the Arduino Uno together.

    Art

    Each dance was timed to match the length of its corresponding Morse letter, which is why the E dance was a single frame and very easy to miss.

    Before we landed on the blob child as the hunt mascot, the TaMITgotchi was originally going to be a Featherbrain. Once we switched to a more amorphous creature, we suddenly had a lot more flexibility in what it could do. We attempted to start with reasonable choreography, but it slowly devolved into some very...interesting dance moves. Dance move D occurred at peak sleep deprivation.

    Games

    We initially planned to only have one game, while having other achievements that were more straightforward, e.g. select a burger to feed the TaMITgotchi. In turn, if you didn't feed your TaMITgotchi then it would die of starvation.

    After a lot of brainstorming, we pivoted to having more games and less death to increase the fun-ness. Ali helped us hone in on these guiding principles:

    • Take advantage of the physical-ness of the device.
    • Emphasis on: implied action → encouragement → delightful discovery. For example,
      • Implied action: a progress bar is under the egg.
      • Encouragement: the progress bar is filling up.
      • Delightful discovery: A blob child hatched out of the egg!
    • Builds up to a finale that requires solvers to apply what they've learned along the way.

    Once we landed on game mechanics we liked, Linda storyboarded the game design on Google slides with simulated buttons on how to navigate the game. It gave us a clear sense of layout and direction before we started coding.

    Although morse code was technically on the "banned mechanics" list at this point because of overrepresentation in our hunt, we managed to convince our editors that it was worth doing because the device would actually be beeping (as opposed to a visual representation). This choice actually predated the kickoff skit creation which also made use of morse - a happy coincidence solidifying the blob child's communication methods as canon.

    Proto1 Hardware Development

    Initial part selection was frantic. The screen was decided on first because we needed something easily source-able and well supported. The detriment of this screen however was that it was much more power hungry than a typical LCD that is in a traditional Tamagotchi. This meant we needed something that could power this screen and a microcontroller. We did some back of the envelope math and realized that we probably needed 2 AA batteries to have a healthy margin on something that would last throughout Hunt. (Initially there was a hope to power things off a typical coin cell, but these would almost certainly die during the hunt). As alluded to above as well, we needed something that would have enough flash to hold all the Art Assets on the device. After consulting with some folks, we decided that something in the STM32 family would be most appropriate for this task, specifically the part we found had 192KB of flash storage compared to the 32KB of flash on the Arduino Uno, and also featured a USB Bootloader ROM. It also was able to be powered off of 2 AA batteries without voltage regulation. Verifying this was the microcontroller we wanted was a mad dash due to the fact the Hardware Lead for the Proto1 development and beyond (Ben) would have to disappear for work reasons for about a month. The STM32 Nucleo evaluation board was rush ordered and the bootloader and screen compatibility were all tested within a week before making the call that this was the part we wanted to use.

    Following Ben's trip, the first board design was pushed out in about a week and sent off to be ordered. And all the parts arrived in Mid August a few weeks before the fall retreat. Ben greatly over estimated how hard it would be to hand solder the MCUs on the board, and had to pivot. He ended up buying a toaster oven to reflow solder the boards. Yield was particularly bad because also initially he had soldered a few boards with the MCU in the wrong orientation (skill issue recognizing the markings on the chip). There were multiple scares of the prototypes not working due to shorts in the soldering job, but ultimately we ended up with 6 prototypes for the fall retreat.

    Lastly, it was kind of an afterthought, but the cases designed for these Proto1s were rushed out in a day. They were 3D printed.

    Scrapped Ideas

    Before fall retreat there were a few things we ended deciding not to do:

    • We didn't want anything to die in the game like typical Tamagotchi. We figure the structure of the game would motivate teams to finish the puzzle content in a reasonable amount of time. The target solve time for the puzzle had to be less than 90 minutes so it wouldn't have made sense to have a dying mechanic after that.
    • Despite leaving a few LEDs on the first prototypes, we ultimately scrapped any additional LEDs. We even had an idea that we would hide an infrared LED that could blink out a signal of some sort.
    • We also wanted to maybe hide some text on the PCB, but ultimately didn't want people disassembling the device during Hunt leading to other issues.
    • Initially, we proposed a more complex extraction that required solvers to input patterns into the device to recreate dances. In the end, we chose to simplify the extraction and instead focus our effort on polishing the games and overall interaction.
    • During the fall retreat, we had also considered "Flappy Bird" as one of our games, but ultimately decided to replace it with the sleep game (turning the lights all off).

    Fall Retreat

    We were ultimately able to use the Proto 1's for our first test solves of the puzzle aspect of the TaMIT during our Fall Retreat. During this retreat we were able to test, walking to hatch the device, the food game, the dance mechanic and extraction. We also separately tested the drinking game on a separate dedicated unit. We learned from this retreat that there was a temptation to just immediately walk and stare at the screen when handed the TaMIT. This unfortunately would damp out all the vibrations so the sensor would not register steps. This led to the idea of providing bags on lanyards and asking people to take the devices just far enough away from HQ to register some progress on the step count to hatch.

    The big scare that occurred during the retreat was however a short event that occurred as we were testing steps and suddenly the device got really hot. This failure was root caused to the throughhole pins of a few components puncturing the insulation of the battery and causing a short. This led to the final design to have very few throughhole components. The initial design also did not feature any reverse polarity protection on the batteries, and some of the devs on the project were very worried about inserting the batteries wrong. This led to the inclusion of a reverse polarity protection circuit on Proto2 and beyond.1

    The last thing we learned at fall retreat was about the maturity of some of the AI coding tools that exist now. At this point we had a clear idea for the food game, drinking game, and poop game, but were less sure about the remaining games. One of the ideas was "flappy bird". Though we ultimately didn't go ahead with it, we were very surprised that with a basic prompt we had a flappy bird prototype within minutes. Later development of the game logic leaned heavily into the use of AI tools to write the firmware. It would not be exaggeration to say we might not have finished all the games had it not been for AI coding tools.

    Proto2, Kingdom Round Testsolve, and Supply Chain Woes

    After the successful solve and the positive signals we got from fall retreat we needed to quickly finalize the design. For Proto2 we took some learnings from the first prototype and did the following:

    1. Added reverse polarity protection on the batteries
    2. Removed a bunch of extra switches for reset and bootloader selector in favor of the final “hold down A button during boot” to select the bootloader
    3. Removed extra LEDs
    4. Shrunk the overall footprint of the board

    For the second run we wanted to attempt a small batch of boards fully assembled for the Kingdom Round testsolve slated for mid October. There was some back and forth with our vendor, and ultimately we agreed that they would not assemble the screens and we would source and solder the screens ourselves. After the order went out we realized it'd take over an estimated 35 days for the boards to arrive. This learning became really important because we needed to put the final bulk order of 150 units >35 days from when we needed to do final assembly before the winter holidays. For Proto2 to meet the Kingdom Round testsolve, we needed to hand build these units again. Again because of soldering skill issues our yield was not great, only about 7/10 boards ultimately worked.

    This time more thought was also put into the design of the cases as well. We knew that assembly had to be easy, so the cases were designed to be snap fit together in halves. Iterating on this was a challenge because we didn't know exactly what tolerances were needed, and the snaps would often break because they were in the plane of the 3D print. With some consultation, we arrived at the current round snap design. Because we reduced the footprint of the board, we were able to also reduce the footprint of the enclosure cases.

    By this point finding testsolvers victims for the puzzle became also a challenge because many team members became "collateral damage" spoiled. This is because the TaMIT was frequently worked on in in-person puzzlehunt working sessions in the Bay Area. Ali worked miracles to gather a fully unspoiled group of testsolvers who could all meet in person at the same time.

    Getting the games together for the Kingdom Round testsolve was also a frantic dash, but we ultimately got a minimum viable product of all the games in order as well as the research task components all working. After a clean full solve with all the games, we were confident in finalizing that this puzzle would work and started to put it in the final order and prepared for the final production of them.

    Final Tests, Supply Chain Panic, and Final Assembly

    We conducted 3 more testsolves of the puzzle each time incrementally improving the UI/UX. One testsolve in particular though had a particular incident. This led to additional instructions to not drop the device. During a testsolve the lanyard was not properly secured to the device, and a testsolver attempted to swing the device on the lanyard causing the device to fly into a wall and the entire assembly kind of exploded.

    The other thing was we discovered that the solve times of the puzzle were taking too long, greater than 90 minutes. We ultimately reduced the number of wins required for each achievement dance to 4 down from 5, and we made the cool down period 2 minutes rather than 3 minutes before the blob child was needy again.

    We got the order for the final 150 boards out in mid October and the estimated time of arrival was going to be around Thanksgiving. This would have offered a decent buffer of about a month to finish all the assembly in December before the winter holidays. Unfortunately our supplier hadn't kept good tabs of sourcing parts and didn't realize some parts didn't arrive. This wasn't discovered until a few days before Thanksgiving. This meant the date was definitely going to slip for when boards would arrive.

    Separately, we needed to 3D print 150+ cases for each of the boards. We originally wanted to split the printing effort, but the other team mates that could load balance would have needed to ship 3D printed parts to the west coast. We did the back of the envelope math and it seemed to be possible for Ben to 3D print enough cases on his Prusa Mk3. However this assumed ~100% yield and this 7 year old printer was running into print failures and it soon became clear that he wouldn't be able to hit the number of printed cases needed by the final assembly date. Around Black Friday, he took advantage of a deal and purchased a new second 3D printer to pick up the slack. This meant we went from on average 6 cases/day to 12 cases/day.

    Because the boards from our supplier were delayed, a truly chaotic week ensued the week the boards did finally arrive. On top of the late arrival, Ben, who was to oversee the assembly both had just returned from a trip to Asia and needed to fly to Chicago the weekend of December 20th. The only weekend that was available to do final assembly had to happen on the 13th and the 14th.

    Transport and Hunt

    As hunt approached, we realized we would need to get all of the assembled TaMITgotchis from the Bay Area to Boston… without freaking out TSA. We fortunately had enough folks traveling early that we were able to have people check them as a bag, and we also researched some backup overnight shipping options with FedEx as fallbacks. Unfortunately we weren't able to fit them all in one bag because it turns out 340 AA batteries weigh a considerable amount and with the rest of the TaMIT materials exceeded the 50 lb limit on 1 bag. But most were able to be brought by one person.

    When the hunt finally arrived we were a bit worried that there would be some technical glitches with the device. Because the puzzle was unlocked so early on, we encountered a rush of people picking up TaMITgotchis initially, and eventually the demand slowed down. In total we ended up handing out 105 units, and to our knowledge there were no defects in the field. It was incredibly rewarding to see teams enjoy the device and a source of great pride that there were no defects.

    In Ben's professional career, he has not been able to actually ship any of the projects he has worked on thus far, and views this as the first thing he has "shipped".
