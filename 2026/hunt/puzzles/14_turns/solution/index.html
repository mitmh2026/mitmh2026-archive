    Solution to ¼ Turns

    Answer: TRIBE
    Written by: Joshua Wang and Ling-Ling Zhang
    Edited by: Max Chang

    Getting Started

    The puzzle consists of six independent matches where launchers attempt to fire rockets at each other to collect bounties. The easiest match by far is match 4, which is one-dimensional and is hence useful for confirming that puzzlers are interpreting the instructions correctly before actually writing formulas or code. The launchers are evenly spaced here, and are at integer coordinates at integer times. The pattern of launcher positions and targets here strongly suggests that the glitch in this puzzle transforms points of the form (0, y) into (0, y - 6.110°). Later on, the fact that all launchers move at unit velocity can be used to confirm that the glitch generally transforms points of the form (x, y) into (x, y - 6.110°).

    The first key insight is that coordinates in this puzzle have been marked with degree symbols (°) because the matches all take place on spheres. The given coordinates represent latitude and longitude (the range of values that each coordinate takes rules out polar coordinates). Hence, in order to figure out which rockets hit which launchers, we will need to do some spherical geometry.

    Spherical Geometry

    First, we would like to compute where a given launcher is at a given time. We do so by first converting from lat-long to 3D Cartesian coordinates via a formula like (lat, lon) → (cos lat cos lon, cos lat sin lon, sin lat) (remember to convert from degrees to radians before plugging into most sin/cos functions). Applying this transformation to the point at time zero and the point at time ninety yields two vectors p_0 and p_{90}. Since the trajectory takes the shortest path on a sphere, we wind up tracing out a great circle and we can interpolate using the formula p_t = (cos t) p_0 + (sin t) p _{90} (again, convert t from degrees to radians before plugging into most sin/cos functions). Finally, we can convert p_t = (x, y, z) back to lat-long using lat = arcsin(z) and lon = atan2(y, x).

    Next, we would like to compute whether a point is within the blast radius of a missile’s target. The blast radius is also in degrees. This boils down to computing distances on a sphere, which can be done using the Haversine Formula. The distances in this puzzle are not adversarial and can be eyeballed instead.

    If we write down a matrix for every match whose (i, j)-th entry has a 1 iff launcher i would hit launcher j if it was still operational, then we would arrive at the following six hit matrices:

    Linear Time

    Given the hit matrices, it is easy to compute what happens as time unfolds linearly from time zero onwards. If a launcher fires, it collects bounties on any launchers hit for the first time (possibly its own bounty). If the bounty is strictly positive, then we get one or two letters from the bounty wheel. We write these letters in order of launch time and each match gives us a single word, read together : CLOSED TIMELIKE CIRCLE FIND STABLE LOOP.

    Circular Time

    Searching the linear-time clue phrase should lead us to closed timelike curves and stable time loops, indicating that the time situation is actually a little more complicated than the linear model we just used. In fact, time here is circular, and time 360 wraps back to time 0. There are a variety of clues towards this being the case:

    • the first half of the puzzle was made difficult by space being on a sphere rather than a plane, and similarly the second half is about time being on a circle rather than a line
    • since all launchers continue to move at unit velocity, no matter whether they are hit or not, they all return to their original positions at exactly time 360
    • the phrase “CLOSED TIMELIKE CIRCLE” specifically replaces the more standard curve with CIRCLE
    • the given points in spacetime (the coordinates for time 0 and the coordinates for time 90) now represent both a ¼ turn in space (around a great circle) and in time (around the 360-long time loop)
    • the bounty wheel wraps around to make 30 equivalent to 1.

    The sequences of events that played out in linear time were not stable; there were often situations where some launchers hit other launchers before being hit in turn. If that state of launchers were to pass into a second iteration of the loop, then such launchers would be disabled and unable to hit the launchers that they did in the first iteration. In fact, things look even more wrong if we try to rewind time and reason about a zeroth iteration.

    We circumvent these paradoxes by solving for a stable time loop: an assignment of enabled/disabled to all launchers such that the following properties hold:

    • every launcher hit by an enabled launcher is disabled
    • every disabled launcher is hit by at least one enabled launcher

    All matches are designed such that there is a unique (stable) assignment. There are a couple of observations that make it easy to solve for this assignment, e.g. a launcher that hits itself must be disabled, a launcher that no other launcher can hit must be enabled. Worst-case, there are always few enough launchers in each match that the assignment can simply be brute-forced over by code.

    In fact, these unique assignments have the additional property that every disabled launcher is hit by exactly one enabled launcher. This additional property means that there is no bounty confusion: it is always clear who should be collecting the bounty for each disabled launcher. Repeating the previous bounty wheel process now yields the clue phrase RANK BETWEEN FAMILY AND GENUS FIVE which solves to TRIBE.
