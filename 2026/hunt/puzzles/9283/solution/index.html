    Combination further details

    Now, let's investigate the combination process. We know the outputs are amino acids; we do not yet know the inputs are amino acids. But we do know that the outputs seem to often match the inputs. Indeed, numbers, which we hypothesized are one of the inputs, often show up in the dropdown section output. More clearly, if we have any of the letters in the example section input, we will see them often show up in that section's output in the corresponding position. Since the outputs often match the inputs, the combination process should work in a way where that is likely, and this makes the most sense if the inputs are also amino acids.

    Thinking more about what makes sense with amino acid inputs and outputs, with the constraints that the process involves randomness and that the output is sometimes one of the inputs, we might conclude that the combination process is as follows:

    • Step 1: Find a codon (remember that a codon is 3 bases) for each input
    • Step 2: Combine the codons into another codon
    • Step 3: Convert the resulting codon to an amino acid

    (Note that we can take codons to be RNA or DNA, i.e. use U or T, and as we will see in this solution, either works. We will use RNA (i.e. use U) in examples in the solution, since it's more common.)

    There is only one obvious way to do Step 3; just (deterministically) use the codon-to-amino-acid table. For Steps 1 and 2 we will first explain what they do, and then explain how we might determine this from the data.

    Step 1

    Step 1 finds a codon by choosing uniformly randomly from the available options (i.e., all options are equally likely). If both inputs are the same amino acid, the two codons chosen may differ anyway.

    For example, if an input codon is S, since S is UCA, UCC, UCG, UCU, AGC, or AGU, each of these will be chosen 1/6th of the time. If an input codon is M, since M is always AUG, AUG will always be chosen.

    As for how to determine this, we should expect there to be randomness in Step 1 (find a codon for both inputs) since sometimes, as mentioned above, 1 + 1 is not all 1 but is mostly all 1. Indeed, assume there were no randomness in Step 1. If Step 2, when combining a codon with itself, did not usually result in that codon (e.g., if it randomly permuted base order, or had frequent mutations), then we would expect 1 + 1 to have many more things other than 1. If Step 2, when combining a codon with itself, always resulted in that codon, then we would expect 1 + 1 to be entirely 1. There are still options such as that Step 2 involves infrequent mutations, or that Step 1 somehow selects a codon differently for its first and second inputs, but the simplest explanation seems to be that Step 1 is random. Given that Step 1 is random, the above description is the simplest option.

    Step 2

    Step 2, for each of the three output codon positions, picks uniformly randomly between the bases in that position in the two input codons (thus giving 8 total overall options for the output codon given the two input codons). For example, if the input codons are AGC and UAG, the eight (equally likely) outputs are AGC, AGG, AAC, AAG, UGC, UGG, UAC, and UAG (the order given here doesn't matter). This is somewhat clued by the title "Recombination," which is a vaguely similar phenomenon.

    As for how to determine this, we know that Step 2 doesn't always choose one of the input codons, since then the output would always be one of the inputs. But what exactly it does do is less clear. Our best option is to guess the above description of Step 2 based on the title and based on the limited number of reasonable options for this step. As will be described, if we look at our outputs, then we can confirm this guess.

    However, if we have enough letters, using the example section can help us figure this out rather than just confirm it. For example, if we look at the first position of the example output, which is obtained by combining the first characters S + M of the example input, and get several hundred samples, we see that the result can be F, I, L, M, R, S, or T. (In the 20 example lines above, the first position values we see are SLIILTISLTRTIISSSSTM, which contain all of these other than F.) S is UCA, UCC, UCG, UCU, AGC, or AGU, while M is always AUG. All of the possible combination results have at least one codon starting with A or U. Furthermore, some at-first-glance possible results such as UGA () never appear (because the first-position U and second-position G can each come only from different S codons). Thinking about what process could lead to these observations can help us find the correct process. However, this requires a lot of letters, so unless we are solving this puzzle very late in the round, we will probably need to come up with the idea for Step 2 and test it, rather than derive it from data.

    Confirming Steps 1 and 2

    Having come up with the above ideas for Steps 1 and 2, (which together determine the entire process), we can try to confirm it. To summarize, the overall combination mechanism is to randomly choose a codon for each of the two input amino acids, randomly choose between the bases of these two codons for each of the three base positions, and interpret the resulting codon as an amino acid. The easiest way to confirm this is to use the x + x data. We can see that most amino acids, when recombined with themselves under this mechanism, always produce themselves. However, there are some exceptions:

    • L + L gives F instead 1/18 of the time
    • R + R gives S instead 1/18 of the time
    • S + S is the most complicated case; it can give many different amino acids, and it gives something other than S 1/4 of the time overall (1/9 T, 1/12 C, 1/36 R, 1/72 W, 1/72 )
    • + gives W instead 1/18 of the time

    (We should make sure we understand how this can happen. For example, for L + L, L can be CUA, CUC, CUG, CUU, UUA, or UUG. 1/9 of the time, the first L selected is CUC or CUU and the second is UUA or UUG, and 1/4 of the time when that happens (so 1/36 total), U is taken from the second for first position and C/U is taken from the first for third position, giving UUC or UUU both of which are F. Another 1/36 of the time, the same thing happens but with the two selected L's reversed (i.e. the first is UUA or UUG and the second is CUC or CUU). The analyses for R + R, S + S, and + are similar.)

    Looking at cases where x + x gives something other than x in the output data, we see that the third position of 1 + 1 follows the R + R distribution, the fifth position of 1 + 1 follows the S + S distribution, and the third position of 2 + 2 follows the L + L distribution. Of course, if we don't have the right letters, we can't tell L + L, R + R, and + apart (though being in any of the inputs 1, 2, or 3 would be surprising). But even in this case, we can still tell S apart because we can see the higher frequency of � and also the occasional , and the 1/4 and 1/18 probabilities should be close enough to what we see in our data to confirm that we're doing the right thing.

def amino_merge_fracs(s1, a, s2, b):
    l = [CODE_TO_ACID[''.join(k)]
    for i in ACID_TO_CODE[a] for j in ACID_TO_CODE[b] for k in product(*zip(i, j))]

    • Letter 1 of 1 + 3: not unique [('K', 'M'), ('L', 'M'), ('M', 'K'), ('M', 'L'), ('M', 'T'), ('M', 'V'), ('T', 'M'), ('V', 'M')]
    • Letter 2 of 1 + 3: unique [('A', 'Y'), ('Y', 'A')]
    • Letter 3 of 1 + 3: unique [('C', 'R'), ('R', 'C')]
    • Letter 4 of 1 + 3: unique [('C', 'I'), ('I', 'C')]
    • Letter 5 of 1 + 3: unique [('E', 'S'), ('S', 'E')]
    • Letter 6 of 1 + 3: not unique [('I', 'N'), ('N', 'I'), ('N', 'T'), ('T', 'N')]

    • Letter 2 of 2 + 3: not unique [('A', 'A'), ('C', 'C'), ('D', 'D'), ('E', 'E'), ('F', 'F'), ('G', 'G'), ('H', 'H'), ('I', 'I'), ('K', 'K'), ('M', 'M'), ('N', 'N'), ('P', 'P'), ('Q', 'Q'), ('T', 'T'), ('V', 'V'), ('W', 'W'), ('Y', 'Y')]

    Overall, the result of the code is that in most cases, even ignoring probabilities, there is only one pair of letters that can give each possible set of results:

    Combined pairLetter 1Letter 2Letter 3Letter 4Letter 5Letter 6
    1 + 2M/PA/YL/Ralways 1 or 2 (*)I/S(1 = 2) (*)
    1 + 3M/KLTV (*)A/YC/RC/IE/SN/IT (*)
    2 + 3P/V(2 = 3) (*)C/LC/ME/IN/IT (*)

    The exceptions (marked by * in the above table) are, in more detail

    • Letter 4 of 1 + 2 is always 1 or 2. There are many options.
    • Letter 6 of 1 + 2 is always (1 = 2). This only tells us they're the same (and likely not something that would sometimes give another result, like L, R, S, or ).
    • Letter 1 of 1 + 3 is 1, 3, or I. This ends up meaning that the inputs are M and one of K, L, T, or V, but we can't tell which.
    • Letter 6 of 1 + 3 is 1, 3, or K. This ends up meaning that the inputs are N and one of I or T, but we can't tell which.
    • Letter 2 of 2 + 3 is always (2 = 3). As before, this only tells us they're the same.
    • Letter 6 of 2 + 3 is 2, 3, or K. As with letter 6 of 1 + 3, this ends up meaning that the inputs are N and one of I or T, but we can't tell which.

    Of course, when we combine x and y, we can't directly tell which letter of a pair comes from x and which comes from y without using probabilities. However, by cross-comparing different pairs, we can usually determine this; we can do this cross-comparison by hand. (We could also write slightly more code for this, but we don't need to.) For example, for letter 3, we know 1 + 2 = L + R, 1 + 3 = C + R, and 2 + 3 = C + L. So for letter 3, 1 = R, 2 = L, and 3 = C. Similarly, for letter 5, 1 + 2 = I + S, 1 + 3 = E + S, and 2 + 3 = E + I. So for letter 5, 1 = S, 2 = I, and 3 = E.

    Letters 1 and 4 are not much worse. For letter 1, 1 + 2 = M + P and 2 + 3 = P + V, so for letter 1, 1 = M, 2 = P, and 3 = V, which is consistent with our data for letter 1 of 1 + 3. For letter 4, 1 + 3 = C + I and 2 + 3 = C + M, so for letter 4, 1 = I, 2 = M, and 3 = C, which is again consistent with our data for letter 4 of 1 + 2.

    Unfortunately, though we can fully disambiguate letters 2 and 6 by looking at probabilities, we cannot do so without probabilities. Without probabilities, we can say for letter 2 that since 1 + 2 = A + Y, 1 + 3 = A + Y, and 2 = 3, either 1 = A, 2 = Y, 3 = Y or 1 = Y, 2 = A, 3 = A. Letter 6 is even worse; all three combinations had ambiguity, so all we know is that 1 = I/T, 2 = I/T, 3 = N (with 1 and 2 being the same one of I/T) or 1 = N, 2 = N, 3 = I/T.

    At this point (assuming we don't use probabilities) we can do Nutrimatic queries such as m[ay]ris[int]__, p[ay]lmi[int]__, and v[ay]cce[int]__. The most promising final two letters taking into account all three of these searches is "ic," with the words being "myristic," "palmitic," and "vaccenic."

    (Note that these words are obscure enough that some other word search tools don't have them all; for example, Qat is missing "vaccenic." Note also that we cannot substitute "ic" for "yl" because then L + L would sometimes be F; even if we thought that the "(x = y)" message was special-cased to avoid this, when combining L + L as part of e.g. 1 + 1 we would notice some non-1 entries.)

    As mentioned above, the line of text at the bottom of the puzzle is "1, 2, or 3, for example (5 4)." Substituting our words in, this becomes "myristic, palmitic, or vaccenic, for example (5 4)." Looking these up, we will find they are all fatty acids. The answer to the puzzle, thematically, is FATTY ACID.

    This puzzle was originally created as a series of pie charts showing probabilities. Thanks to Herman for suggesting making it interactive instead, which I think makes it far more interesting (since you can't distinguish different letters that are all �).

    I didn't originally intend this puzzle to require coding, but it turns out that combining amino acids in the way this puzzle uses is hard by hand. Testsolves generally started by trying to solve by hand, but all or almost all ended up writing code. At least the code is not that hard to write.

    We (I/Herman/testsolvers) were unsure whether the enumeration was needed. After discussion, due to the number of other plausible answers without it (ACID, ACIDS, FATTY ACIDS) we decided to give it rather than including all these partials and maybe missing some other plausible partial.
