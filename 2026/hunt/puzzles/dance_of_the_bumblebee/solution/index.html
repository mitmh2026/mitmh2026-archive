    Solution to Dance of the Bumblebee

    Answer: INTERNATIONAL AIRPORT
    Written by: Sam Wang
    Edited by: Herman Chau
    Additional thanks to:
    • Annie Guo for ideation of quotations and testing
    • Herman Chau for glyph art and ideation
    • Max Chang for implementation

    This puzzle features a conlang from a world where sentient bees write down their communications using glyphs on hexagons. Solvers are expected to slowly learn the grammar and some useful words from the Bee language via example sentences and eventually understand and even write their own complex sentences.

    This solution provides a lot of information for comprehensiveness. The reader may find certain sections more useful:

    • The Level Layout section gives a runthrough of the puzzle's presentation and terminology, but should not be needed for anyone who has spent more than a few minutes looking through the puzzle.
    • (Recommended) The Language Primer section will provide a crash-course to how the language works - after reading this (and importing the Full Lexicon), most of the sentences in the level should be understandable to some degree.
    • The Advanced Grammar section covers the remaining grammar points, each of which are used in only some levels and/or not essential to the meaning of the sentences that they are used in.
    • Intended solve paths for each individual level have also been provided. Each level is labeled with a letter for ease of reference, as well their complexity tier number, with T1 being the easiest and T3 being the hardest. Lower tier levels provide many more examples and simpler grammar. This puzzle has been laid out so that levels require knowledge from earlier tiered levels, but none from levels in the same or higher tier. The solutions for each level will thus be presented as if the solver has not solved any other level of the same or higher tier.
    • Full Lexicon contains the glosses for every word that appears in the puzzle and a list of all the semantic groups.
    Level Layout

    Each level features three components:

    • Example tile: Some number of given tiles with a 💡 that can be clicked to reveal an overlay. The overlay will contain one or more groupings of glyphs, representing words or sentences in the conlang. Most Example tile overlays will also include images that essentially clue the meaning of the words or sentences in some fashion. Example tiles in later rounds may omit images entirely, instead using only previously-learned vocabulary to explain new words.
    • Prompt sentence with Clue tile: A collections of mostly blank tile slots adjacent to a single 👁‍🗨 Prompt tile. The Prompt tile can be clicked to reveal an overlay. The Prompt tile overlay shows some images and/or sentences, and the solver must fill out the blank tiles (with tiles from the tile bank) to construct a phrase or sentence that best "answers" the prompt in the Prompt tile. In easier levels, imagine the Prompt tile overlay just shows an image of a hand holding a cup - the blank tiles are expected to be filled with a conlang phrase meaning "hand holding cup". Some blank tile slots may have tiles pre-filled for especially tricky grammar.
      Each level has 2-3 of these blank sentences. All of them must be filled out correctly to successfully submit the level.
    • Lexicon tile: A tile with a 📖 icon that can be clicked to reveal an overlay. The Lexicon tile overlay shows a global view of every single tile that the solver has "unlocked" i.e. either shows up in a Lightbulb tile, the word bank, or pre-filled in a blank sentence. Note that all the Lexicon tiles in every level open up the exact same overlay, since it's all just shared. There's one in each level just for convenience.

    Language primer

    Overall design goal

    Each glyph represents one distinct word, and most grammar concepts e.g. tense, person, plurality are represented by combining glyphs in a certain order instead of adding prefixes/suffixes/modifying the glyph.

    Each glyph is connected grammatically to at least one adjacent glyph. The exact direction that glyph X is relative to glyph Y is meaningful for how X and Y relate grammatically.

    Glyph classes (Parts of speech)

    Bee glyphs have one of three basic "glyph classes": nouns, verbs, and connectives. Nouns and verbs generally go under the noun and verb glyph classes, whereas many English prepositions / function words fall under connectives. Other English parts of speeches (e.g. adverbs/adjectives) can be defined as either a special subcase of one of these glyph classes or as a combination of multiple glyphs using different glyph classes.

    Glyph class primarily constrain where the word are physically located in a sentence, relative to other words. E.g. nouns are always in the DL or DR direction from a verb they are related to, and whether it is DL or DR indicates exactly the grammatical relationship with each other.

    Noun+verb

    As mentioned above, nouns always go below verbs. A noun in the DL direction from a verb is the one doing or experiencing the verb (henceforth called the subject), and a noun in the DR direction from a verb is the one being acted upon by the verb (henceforth called the direct object). In the example below, "person" is the subject and "bird" is the direct object.

    This corresponds to English subject/direct object in most cases, with the notable exception being passive voice. E.g. in a sentence like "the bird was eaten", English treats "bird" as the subject, but the Bee language considers "bird" as the object of the verb "eats":

    Subjects and direct objects are not always necessary, and can be omitted. This subject was "omitted" for the "passive" sentence above. In a sentence like "the person sleeps", the verb simply doesn't need a direct object:

    Adjectives are a special case of a verb that always takes exactly one subject and no direct object. Instead of having a word "blue", the adjective would be some verb "is blue" representing the state of "being blue":

    Connectives

    Connectives act as linking words between the other two glyph types. These fill the roles of prepositions and linking words like "that", "if", etc. in English. Consider the phrase "snow on the mountain" in English. Here, "on" is a preposition that links two other words: the noun "snow" and the noun "mountain". A little more technically, "snow" is being modified by prepositional phrase "on the mountain", which acts like a description. We can then label "snow" as the "modified" noun and "mountain" as the "complement" noun (i.e. complemented "on" to form a descriptive phrase).

    Connectives in the Bee language work similarly. "on" would be a connective glyph, with the modified noun glyph "snow" to the UR and the complement glyph "mountain" to the UL:

    Note that verbs can also be used with connectives! In a phrase like "goes on the road", "on" would be a connective glyph, with the modified verb glyph "goes" to the DR and the noun glyph "road" to the UL.

    As can be gleaned in the examples above, a few rules govern the positioning of the glyphs connected by the connective. Connected nouns are always located above the connective. Connected verbs are always located below the connective. Complement glyphs are always located to the left of the connective, and modified glyphs are always located to the right of the connective. In a sense, the connective "reading order" is backwards from how prepositions are read in English.

    The different ways connectives can be used (with more examples) are covered more in-depth in Advanced Grammar.

    Compounding

    Glyphs of the same two word classes can be connected horizontally to compound them. When glyph A is to the left of glyph B, A is said to be "compounding" on B. Compounding means either one of two things:

    • Combining B and A in some way, e.g. B and A. E.g. compounding the verb glyphs for "studies" and "eats" indicates studying and eating at the same time.
    • A modifies B in some way. For nouns, this could be B with the attribute of A. E.g. "snow" compounding on "person" means a "person" with the attribute of "snow", AKA a snowman.

    More specific details on how each different word class can compound are covered in Advanced Grammar.

    Parse tree

    Excluding single-word sentences, each tile in a sentence must be immediately connected to at least one other tile. At the same time, a tile does not need to to be immediately connected to every adjacent tile.

    The subject "person" and the "bird" are both immediately connected to the verb "eats". However, subject "person" and direct object "bird" are not considered immediately connected. I.e. we are not connecting these two nouns to form a compound "person-bird".

    In fact, sentences must follow the following constraint - between any two tiles of the sentence, there is only one valid path of immediate connections that connects them i.e. the immediate connections between tiles must form a tree structure (no cycles). Part of the challenge of parsing/writing longer sentences is understanding where the connections lie to form a valid parse tree:

    Glyph anatomy

    Central shape

    Glyph classes (covered in the primer) are indicated by the shape in the center of the glyph.

    ShapePart of Speech
    TriangleNoun
    CircleVerb
    SquareConnective

    This is meant to aid in learning grammatical structure and parsing longer sentences - e.g. only "triangles" can be below "circles" and still connect to them, so solvers can parse "circle" glyphs as not connected grammatically to any non-"triangle" glyphs below the "circle" glyphs (as explained in the Glyph Classes section).

    Word class shift

    Two words can look exactly the same other than one has the shape for a noun, and one has the shape of a verb. This indicates the core, fundamental meaning is the same, but expressed as a different word class . An example would be the verb "to die / is dead" and the noun "death" share the same glyph aside from the central shape.

    Radial strokes

    There are up to 6 strokes radiating out of the central shape. The strokes themselves don't carry any inherent meaning - they act like letters of the Bee language's orthography. There are 3 large strokes and 3 small strokes, alternating. The 3 large strokes identify a "semantic group". I.e. if two glyphs share the exact same 3 large strokes (including if a stroke is absent or not) in the same positions, their meanings are related in some way. As an example: The small strokes don't have any pattern, however. Their only purpose is to distinguish between different glyphs within the same semantic group.

    Numbers

    Numbers are base 6. Each side of the hexagon represents a digit:

    Placing an arc with 2 wings indicates multiplying the digit by 61=6. E.g. the following example indicates 6x2+0x1=12:

    In general, an arc with n wings indicates multiplying the digit by 6n-1.

    The digit 0 for a place only needs to be indicated if there are larger places indicated in the number (or for the number 0). This is just like how the Arabic number "42" would not be written with a bunch of leading 0's (e.g. "00042").

    Word class of numbers

    Numbers can be either verbs or nouns. When they are used to describe the quantity of some nouns, numbers are written as verbs, placed to the UR of the noun they are quantifying (this usage is similar to adjectives).

    When numbers are used as a standalone number or symbolically, e.g. as part of the expression "10-4", they are written as nouns. Listing out numbers (as in the same expression "10-4") can be done by combining them with the connective "with" to clearly indicate the separation between the numbers.

    Noun + Connective + Verb

    This is the case with a verb glyph as the complement and a noun glyph as the modified word. This grammar only applies when using the connective "that", which connects the complement and noun like so: The complement verb acts as the root of some independent clause that describes/modifies the noun. Consider the English sentence "I know that the person is sleeping." This can be reworded more explicitly as "I know the fact that the person is sleeping." Here, "the person is sleeping" is an independent clause that modifies "fact". In the Bee language, the verb "sleep" would be the complement, "that" would be the connective, and "fact" (AKA "thing") would be the modified noun:

    This "thing + that" construction is a common building block to many complex sentences with multiple clauses. For instance, the sentence "I walk because the car is dead" could be reworded as "I walk for the reason that the car is dead", where "for" is a connective modifying the verb "walk", and "reason" is written as "thing":

    Compounding (advanced)

    See Compounding section in the Language Primer for the intro on how compounding works.

    This section will dive deeper into the different ways compounding is used for each word class.

    Compounding nouns

    The Language Primer introduced compounding X on Y to mean "X and Y". As a special case, X can be compounded on itself to indicate a plural number (>1) of X. E.g. the following would indicate multiple "birds":

    Note that reduplication is not done when a noun is already specified with a number - the Bee language considers it redundant to indicate plurals the number already specifies "plurarlity". In a sense, reduplication indicates an "indeterminate" plural whereas numbers indicate a "determinate" plural.

    Compounding verbs

    A verb X compounding on verb Y (i.e. X to the left of Y) can have one of the following interpretations:

    • X and Y happening at the same time. E.g. "studies" compounded on <"eats" indicates studying and eating at the same time.
    • Y happens first, THEN X happens. E.g. "the person eats, then sleeps":
    • X is an auxiliary verb modifying Y. "can/able to" and "not" are both auxiliary verbs that use verb compounding. E.g. "the person doesn't eat":

    Compounding connectives

    A verb can be affected by multiple connectives. The second connective would be placed to the left of the first one, as if it was compounding it. E.g. "the car goes on the road to the mountain":

    Multi-compounding

    Note: compounding can be done with three or more glyphs! E.g. if A, B, and C are all nouns, it could be parsed as either:

      A compounded on (B compounded on C)
      (A compounded on B) compounded on C

    Compounding "root"

    The "right-most" glyph in the compound serves as the root for further connections with other word classes. Consider a sentence "The snowman eats". The noun compound here is "snow" + "person", and the "eats" glyph is placed to the UR of "person".

    Full adjacency reference

    This diagram summarizes all 6 ways a glyph from each word class can connect to other glyphs. Note that an individual word does not necessarily accept connections in all 6 directions - e.g. the verb "sleeps" does not accept a direct object.

    Reference tags

    A specific thing or action could be referenced multiple times in the same sentence or even the same level. To aid in understanding, each instance where that thing or action is referenced can be tagged with an a single number glyph.

    E.g. imagine two individual sentences: "The person goes to the mountain." and "She walks on snow."

    The above examples are akin to saying "Person 1 goes to the mountain." and "Person 1 walks on snow."

    Reference tags can also be used to indicate the same action is being referenced. Image the sentences "I can sleep. I know it [=that I can sleep]", perhaps spoken by a struggling insomniac to themselves. This can be written by reference tagging the "sleep" glyph and the "thing" glyph (meaning "fact" or "action" here) with the same reference number:

    The number used to tag a reference is unique for each "thing" being tagged within the level. However, it is not unique between different levels, to avoid confusion with bigger numbers.

    1st/2nd person pronouns

    The 1st/2nd person pronouns ("I/me" and "you") are represented simply as their own noun glyphs. Reference tags are not needed for these pronouns. The word "we" is represented by compounding "you" on "I/me".

    Junctions

    Sometimes glyphs would need to overlap when following the correct directional adjacency rules. To alleviate this, some glyphs simply serve as junctions. The lines indicate which tiles are considered "connected" for the purposes of parsing the sentence:

    Here, "hand" is connected to "holds", as the subject of that verb. Similarly, "card" is connected to "card" - reduplication is used to indicate plurality (multiple "cards").

    Gender

    The vast majority of bees are female, so female is assumed to be the "default". A sentence "the person eats" can describe either a man or a woman, but often assumed to be a woman. To explicitly indicate "a man eats", the noun should be compounded by the noun "male" (borrowed from the word for "drone bee"):

    Imperative sentences

    Imperative sentences are simply regular sentences, but the subject is explicitly written as "you". E.g. "Eat the bird!":

    Omitted grammar

    Commonly used grammatical in English, like articles and tense, are not explicitly represented in the Bee language grammar, instead heavily relying on context or other grammar to fill in the gaps.

    Articles

    The sentences "the cat drinks water" and "a (or some) cat drinks water" would be represented with the same glyphs in the Bee language. If it's needed to emphasize exactly which cat is drinking the water, a reference tag can easily fill the role.

    Tense

    Tense is not generally represented in most sentences of the bee languages. The sentences "the cat drinks water", "the cat drank water", and "the cat will continue to drink water" would all be represented with the same glyphs. When a specific time for something happening is important, e.g. to emphasize "I had a bird 4 years ago", a time connective e.g. "4 years ago" can be used to indicate the timing (and past tense is implied):

    Ambiguity

    The lack of certain grammar features / ambiguous parsing of the adjacency tree can lead to multiple valid interpretations of a sentence. This is something that can happen in all languages, including natural ones. In a lot of cases, context should be enough to disambiguate the meaning (e.g. what is more likely to be said?). In general, each level of the puzzle is designed in a way where there is enough information within the level, based on other example sentences and/or the central theme of the level, for sentences to be parsed correctly.

    Author's Notes

    There was a desire to have a conlang puzzle in this round, partially because we didn't yet have a true conlang puzzle in the hunt, and this round needed puzzles. So I started ideating about ways to make a conlang that felt perfectly at home in a hex grid.

    The Bee theme came about because when thinking of conlangs, it's fun to think of some non-human species that might have their own language, and what species fits a hex grid better than bees?

    Next was looking for a grammar that worked well in a hex grid. Most natural human languages are written in a linear fashion (left to right, right to left, or even top to bottom), but this would be quite boring in a hex grid. Instead, I wanted to have a grammar where the "next word" in the sentence could be potentially placed in any of the 6 adjacent hexes. And to make use of this "multidimension" word order, I wanted to make word order crucial to the grammar - make one wrong mistake in the word order and your sentence means something completely different!

    Sentences that rely on a strict word order tended to be what are called "isolating languages". Basically, the language rarely uses prefixes/suffixes to indicate grammatical relations between words (i.e. conjugating verbs or declining nouns), so they rely on combining words in a meaningful word order. Take English for example - we might say "I eat the chicken with the fork". But mix up your word order - "I eat the fork with the chicken" - and your sentence becomes very different. For this reason, I wanted this conlang to avoid verb conjugations and noun declensions as much as possible. Grammar points like word class shifts ended up being exceptions, but overall I think I was quite successful in this goal.

    In the end, a lot of the remaining grammar features being inspired, sometimes subconciously, by grammatical concepts from English, Mandarin Chinese (another heavily isolating language), and Japanese (languages that I was most familiar with). I hope this actually helped the grammar still feel somewhat intuitive to the majority of solvers, since these are all commonly studied languages. Some (non-exhaustive) examples from each:

    • English: Many of the common connectives were used in the same way as English prepositions.
    • Chinese: Semantic groups were inspired by Chinese radicals. The idea of "one glyph per word" in general. The way some of the noun compounds work.
    • Japanese: adjectives working as verbs (inspired by i-adjectives). Using "thing" modified by a relative phrase to treat the whole relative phrase as a noun, similar to Japanese "koto".

    For puzzle design, the goal was teach a conlang without relying on giving translations or even the grammar upfront, basically in the style of Rosetta Stone software. In some ways this was successful - in the end, none of the teaching material needed to be in English (other than stray English showing up in the image of a dictionary, for example).

    But it has been a challenge to successfully teach some core concepts of the grammar without being able to ground the explanation in English, and the puzzle went through a couple rounds of reworks/nerfs to make a lot of the grammar more accessible.
